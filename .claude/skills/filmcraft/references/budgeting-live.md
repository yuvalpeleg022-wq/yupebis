# Budgeting a film, location-aware and live

How real film budgets are built, and why every money figure in this file must be
pulled fresh before you rely on it. Today is 2026-07-20. Rates, tax-credit
percentages, union scale, and comparable budgets all drift, some of them every
year, some mid-year. Treat any number here as a placeholder shape, not a quote.

The working references behind this doc: "Film + Video Budgets" (Deke Simon and
Michael Wiese), "The Complete Film Production Handbook" (Eve Light Honthaner),
the Movie Magic Budgeting account structure that most line producers actually
use, and the union rate cards published by SAG-AFTRA, IATSE, and the DGA. Line
producers and UPMs (unit production managers) own this document on a real show;
a producer signs it.

## The shape of a budget

A film budget is a spreadsheet with two layers. The **top sheet** is the
one-page summary, each line an account total. Behind each account sits a
**detail page** with the individual line items, quantities, rates, and units
(days, weeks, hourly, flat, allow). You read the top sheet; you defend the
detail.

Accounts are numbered by convention (Movie Magic and the old Hollywood
standard). Rough grouping:

- **1000s — above the line (ATL).** Story and rights, producers, director,
  cast. The named, deal-driven money. Negotiated, not scheduled.
- **2000s–5000s — below the line (BTL), production.** Everything to get the
  image and sound in the can: crew by department, cast support, set
  construction, camera, grip, electric, wardrobe, locations, transportation,
  catering.
- **6000s–7000s — post-production.** Editorial, VFX, sound mix, music,
  color, delivery.
- **8000s — other.** Insurance, legal, financing, general overhead,
  publicity, contingency, completion bond.

The split people quote is ATL vs BTL vs post. ATL is the deal; BTL is the
machine; post is the finish. A studio drama might run 15-25% ATL. A
star-driven tentpole can push ATL far higher because one cast deal moves the
whole sheet. Verify against comparables, below.

### Contingency and the completion bond

**Contingency** is a real line, not a rounding cushion. Standard is around
10% of the below-the-line-plus-post number, sometimes calculated on the whole
budget; a lender or bond company sets the exact basis. It absorbs overages
without a new financing conversation.

**Completion bond** (completion guarantee) is insurance sold by a
guarantor (Film Finances is the historical name) promising financiers the
film gets delivered or their money back. The premium is a percentage of the
budget, often quoted around 2-3% with a rebate if unused, but the number and
the terms are deal-specific — verify before relying. Micro and many indie
films skip the bond; financiers on larger films usually require it.

## Rates: how labor is actually priced

Two structures dominate crew and cast pricing.

- **Day player.** Hired by the day, paid a daily rate, often with defined
  overtime past a base of 8, 10, or 12 hours. Used for actors in a few
  scenes and for crew brought on for specific days.
- **Weekly.** Hired by the week (commonly a 5-day or 6-day week), paid a
  weekly rate that usually bakes in a set number of hours before overtime.
  Department heads and core crew are weekly.

A budget line reads: quantity x rate x time unit, plus fringes. **Fringes**
are the loaded costs on top of wages — payroll taxes, union pension/health/
welfare contributions, workers' comp, payroll-service fee. Fringes are not
optional and not small; on union labor they can add 20-35%+ to raw wage.
Verify the current fringe percentages with your payroll house before relying.

### Union vs non-union

Union work means you sign a collective-bargaining agreement and pay at least
scale (minimum) plus the union's pension and health contributions, and you
follow the work rules (turnaround, meal penalties, minimum calls).

- **SAG-AFTRA** — screen actors. Multiple agreements scaled to budget:
  low-budget tiers (Ultra Low, Modified Low, Low Budget), the Basic
  Agreement for larger films, plus new-media and short-film agreements. The
  budget ceiling for each tier and the daily/weekly scale change — pull the
  current SAG-AFTRA rate sheet at runtime.
- **IATSE** — most below-the-line crew (camera, grip, electric, art,
  wardrobe, editorial, and more) through local unions. Rates vary by local
  and by area standards agreement.
- **DGA** — directors, ADs, UPMs, stage managers. Minimum salaries and
  working conditions by budget tier.
- **Teamsters** — drivers and transportation in covered regions.

**Non-US equivalents.** UK: **BECTU/Prospect** for crew and the **PACT**
agreements between producers and unions; **Equity** for performers. Canada:
**ACTRA** for performers, **IATSE** and **DGC** (Directors Guild of Canada)
for crew and directors. Australia: **MEAA** covering both performers and
crew. India: craft-specific bodies such as the **FWICE** federation and
regional guilds; practice varies widely by state and language industry.
Every one of these publishes rates that move — verify the current card.

Non-union lets you set your own rates and hours, common at micro and indie
tiers, but you lose the standardized protections and you may struggle to
attract experienced crew or union-covered talent.

## Budget by tier

Rough tiers, with the loud caveat that the dollar ranges below drift with
inflation, market, and financing climate and must be re-checked before you
quote them to anyone. As of 2026-07-20 treat these as order-of-magnitude
shapes only, verify before relying.

- **Micro-budget.** Roughly under a few hundred thousand. Skeleton crew,
  favors, non-union or the lowest SAG tier, minimal locations, available
  light. Contingency thin, no bond.
- **Indie.** Roughly low-single-digit to low-double-digit millions.
  Real crew, low-budget union agreements, a real schedule, some contingency,
  a bond if a lender demands one.
- **Mid / studio-specialty.** Roughly the tens of millions. Full union
  crew, guaranteed cast, real post and VFX budgets, full bond and
  insurance.
- **Studio.** Roughly $50-150M range. Full guilds, large departments,
  significant VFX, marketing handled separately (P&A is not in the
  production budget).
- **Tentpole.** $150M+ and up. Heavy VFX, large cast deals, global
  production, extended post. ATL can dominate the sheet.

The pattern that holds across tiers: as budget rises, the share going to
above-the-line deals and to VFX/post tends to rise, and the per-day page
count you can shoot tends to fall because coverage, cast, and setups get more
elaborate.

## Location: costs and incentives

Where you shoot changes the budget twice: the **base cost** of labor,
facilities, and services differs by market, and the **incentive** you can
claw back differs by jurisdiction. Both are live data.

### Film tax credits, rebates, and grants (conceptual)

Jurisdictions compete for production spend by offering money back. The main
mechanisms:

- **Refundable / transferable tax credit.** A percentage of your
  qualifying local spend comes back as a credit. If refundable, the
  government pays the excess over your tax liability; if transferable, you
  sell the credit to a local taxpayer at a discount. Georgia (US) built its
  industry on a transferable credit of this kind.
- **Cash rebate / grant.** A direct percentage rebate on qualifying spend,
  often with a minimum spend threshold and a cap. The UK, various Canadian
  provinces, and Australia have run rebate-style programs.
- **Uplifts.** Extra percentage points for shooting in a specific region,
  hiring local labor, or hitting a cultural test.

Named examples to check live, never to quote from memory:

- **UK** — a spend-based relief for qualifying films (the mechanism and
  headline rate have been reformed recently; pull the current HMRC / BFI
  rules).
- **Georgia, US** — transferable credit with an additional uplift for a
  promotional logo. Rate and rules set by state law.
- **Canada** — federal credit plus stacking provincial credits (Ontario,
  British Columbia, Quebec each differ), often richer for local labor.
- **Australia** — a federal offset for qualifying production plus state
  incentives; thresholds and rate have moved.
- **India** — central incentive for foreign productions plus state-level
  subsidies (several states court production with cash and fee waivers);
  terms vary state to state and change with each policy cycle.

Every rate, cap, minimum-spend threshold, sunset date, and qualifying-spend
definition here is **subject to change and must be pulled live before you
rely on it.** An incentive that existed last year may be capped out,
suspended, or reformed this year. Note today's date (2026-07-20) in any
estimate and cite the source and its date.

### Comparable-film benchmarking

The fastest sanity check on a budget is to line it up against films of the
same genre, scale, and shooting footprint. Sources: trade press (Variety,
The Hollywood Reporter, Deadline) for reported budgets, public financing
disclosures, and incentive-application filings where jurisdictions publish
them. Reported budgets are often rounded or spun — treat them as a range, use
several comps, and label them as reported-not-audited.

## Runnable method: logline + tier + location to a rough top sheet

Inputs you need before you start. If any is missing, ask for it — do not
guess location or currency.

1. **Logline / scope.** Genre, cast size, number of locations, day/night
   mix, VFX load, period or contemporary, rough shoot length in weeks.
2. **Tier.** Micro / indie / mid / studio / tentpole.
3. **Location(s).** Country, state/province, city. Drives base rates,
   currency, and incentive.
4. **Currency.** State it and hold every figure in it.

Then:

**Step 1 — set the ATL/BTL/post/other split from tier and comps.** Pick a
split shape from comparable films at this tier and genre. Label it an
assumption.

**Step 2 — estimate shoot length.** From scope: pages of script (if known)
divided by the per-day page rate for the tier gives shoot days; round to
whole weeks. If no script, estimate days from scene count and location count.

**Step 3 — price the machine.** Crew size scales with tier. Weekly crew cost
= (heads x weekly rate x weeks) + fringe. Add cast (day players + weekly
leads), locations, equipment, transport, catering, art/construction sized to
the genre.

**Step 4 — post and delivery.** Editorial weeks, VFX shot count x per-shot
range, sound mix, music (score vs licensed), color, deliverables.

**Step 5 — load the top.** Insurance, legal, contingency (~10%, verify
basis), completion bond premium if the tier and financier require one.

**Step 6 — apply the incentive.** Estimate qualifying local spend, apply the
jurisdiction's current rate (pulled live), subtract the expected credit/
rebate to show net cost. Show gross and net.

**Step 7 — label everything.** Every figure carries one of: pulled-live
(with source and date), comp-derived (with the comps), or assumption. No
bare numbers.

### Output format

Produce a top-sheet table (account, description, estimate, basis) grouped
ATL / BTL / post / other, a running total, an incentive line showing gross
vs net, then two lists:

- **Assumptions** — every judgment call made, in plain language.
- **Verify before relying** — every figure that must be pulled live before
  this estimate is used for a real decision: current union scale for the
  location, current fringe percentages, current incentive rate/cap/threshold
  and its sunset date, current local crew and equipment rates, and the
  comparable budgets used.

Close every estimate with the date it was made (2026-07-20 today) and the
line that money and incentive figures drift heavily and were not audited.
