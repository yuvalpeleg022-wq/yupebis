# Music, songs and lyrics for film

Working reference for writing songs and lyrics to picture, briefing a composer, and choosing and clearing source music. Today is 2026-07-20; all rights and cost figures drift, so verify before relying on any number here.

## Part A — Songwriting and lyrics for film

### What a song is doing in the scene

A film song is never just a song. It carries a story job, and the job dictates the craft. Name the job before writing a line.

- **I-want song.** States the protagonist's desire out loud so the audience can want it too. "Part of Your World" (Alan Menken / Howard Ashman, *The Little Mermaid*), "Waving Through a Window" (Pasek and Paul, *Dear Evan Hansen*). Structure: verse establishes the cage, chorus names the longing, bridge escalates the stakes. Ashman's rule, passed to the *Aladdin* and *Beauty and the Beast* teams, was that the I-want song sits early in the first act and the whole plot is the character chasing that line.
- **Montage / passage-of-time song.** Covers narrative distance the scene work would drag through. Lyrics stay slightly general so images can float against them; the song does emotional continuity while the cut does the plot. Think "Gonna Fly Now" logic — few words, strong repeated hook, room for picture.
- **Emotional-turn song.** Lands on the beat where a character changes. The lyric should arrive at its most exposed line exactly on the visual turn. Sondheim's "Send in the Clowns" works because the hook is a bitter question, not a statement, so it turns with the character.
- **Item / dance number.** Energy and spectacle first, plot second. In Hindi cinema the "item number" is a standalone high-tempo number, often not sung by a lead character. Craft priority: a hook you can chant, a rhythm built for choreography, repeatable phrases (Gulzar's playful internal rhymes, or the percussive title-drops in many A. R. Rahman dance cuts).
- **End-credits / end-title song.** Sends the audience out carrying the theme. It can restate the film's thesis more plainly than any in-story song, because the story is over. Often a licensed or commissioned pop record aimed at radio and playlists as much as the film.
- **Source / diegetic song.** A song characters can hear (radio, band, bar). It has to feel like it exists in that world's reality — period, genre, region — before it does any authorial work.

### Lyric craft

**Meter and prosody.** Prosody is the fit between word stress and musical stress. The strong syllables of the line must land on the strong beats of the melody, or the singer fights the tune. Sondheim's *Finishing the Hat* and *Look, I Made a Hat* are the standing texts here: he hammers that a wrongly-stressed rhyme ("the AB-solute" falling wrong) reads as amateur no matter how clever the idea. Say the line out loud against the intended rhythm; if you have to squash or stretch a word to make it fit, the line is wrong, not the tune.

**Syllable stress matching a melody.** When you write to an existing melody (common in film, and standard in Indian playback where the tune often exists first), you are counting syllables and matching accents per phrase. Map the melody as a stress pattern (da-DA-da-DA) and fill words whose natural stress matches. In Hindi film work this is the job of the lyricist writing to Rahman's or Pritam's pre-composed tune — Gulzar, Javed Akhtar, Amitabh Bhattacharya, Varun Grover all do this. Grover has described writing "Moh Moh Ke Dhaage" to a fixed tune, choosing soft consonants to sit on the melody's held notes.

**Rhyme scheme and rhyme type.** Perfect rhyme (day/way) reads as closed and formal; slant/near rhyme (home/gone) reads as open and modern. Choose per tone. A tight AABB feels like a nursery or a comic number; ABAB feels like a ballad; a delayed rhyme (rhyme lands two lines later than the ear expects) builds tension. Ashman and Menken used interior rhyme to make patter feel effortless ("Be Our Guest"). Avoid rhyming on filler words (the, and, you) landing on the money beat.

**Hook and refrain.** The hook is the one phrase the audience leaves humming. It should carry the song's meaning in the fewest words, sit on the catchiest melodic figure, and usually contain or imply the title. Repeat it, but earn each repeat by changing what's around it so the same words mean something new by the last chorus.

**Imagery over statement.** Concrete images beat abstract feeling. "I'm sad" is dead; a specific object doing the emotional work is alive. This is why "The Windmills of Your Mind" (Michel Legrand / the Bergmans) lists spinning objects instead of naming its dread.

**Avoiding cliché.** Ban the reflexive rhymes (fire/desire, heart/apart, night/light) unless you subvert them. Cut any line that could belong to any song. Test: would this line survive if the character's name and situation changed? If yes, it's too generic. Dorothy Fields and Johnny Mercer are worth studying for plain, specific American diction that never reaches for the poetic word when the true word is smaller.

### Writing in different traditions

**Hollywood musical / integrated Broadway.** In an integrated score (Rodgers and Hammerstein onward) a character sings because speech has run out of voltage; the song advances plot and reveals character, and the scene is different after the song than before. The Ashman model for Disney animation is Broadway craft applied to film: clear song function, act structure, reprises that recontextualize.

**Bollywood / Indian film song.** The dominant structure is **mukhda–antara**. The *mukhda* is the opening hook-refrain (the "face" of the song, carrying the title line); the *antara* is the verse that departs melodically and lyrically then returns to the mukhda. A song typically runs mukhda → antara 1 → mukhda → antara 2 → mukhda. Songs are almost always **playback**: recorded by playback singers (historically Lata Mangeshkar, Kishore Kumar, Mohammed Rafi; now Arijit Singh, Shreya Ghoshal and others) and lip-synced on screen by actors. This changes the writing — you write for a singer's voice and an actor's body at once. The music director (Rahman, Pritam, Shankar–Ehsaan–Loy, Vishal–Shekhar) usually composes the tune first; the lyricist fits words to it. Ghazal-influenced lyricists (Gulzar, Sahir Ludhianvi historically, Javed Akhtar) bring Urdu poetic diction; newer writers (Amitabh Bhattacharya, Varun Grover, Irshad Kamil) write more colloquial Hindi. Regional cinemas (Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Bengali) each have their own song grammar and star lyricists (Vairamuthu, Vaali, Sirivennela Seetharama Sastry).

**Multi-language lyric and singable translation.** A singable translation is not a literal translation. You must preserve, in priority order: (1) singability — same syllable count and stress pattern per phrase, open vowels on high sustained notes; (2) the hook's meaning and position; (3) rhyme feel; (4) literal sense, which is the first thing you sacrifice. This is Peter Low's "pentathlon principle" for song translation (singability, sense, naturalness, rhythm, rhyme). For dubbing songs across languages, the constraint tightens to lip-sync on close-ups. Never translate the hook word-for-word if the natural-language version breaks the stress — refit it.

### Worked example — write the actual lyric

**Brief.** Emotional-turn song, roughly 40 seconds of screen time, English, folk-ballad register. Scene: a woman stands on a station platform having decided not to board the train that would take her back to a safe, small life. The turn is from fear to release. She is the POV character. Melody assumed: a gentle 6/8 lilt, four-line phrases, the third line lifting to the highest note.

**The lyric (one verse and refrain):**

> The nine-fifteen is pulling out, I let it take my coat,
> the folded map, the ironed days, the note I never wrote.
> Go on and keep the quiet house, the clock I used to wind —
> I'm staying with the open door and the year I left behind.

**Craft choices, line by line.**

- *POV and function.* Every image is hers and physical (coat, map, clock, door). The song's job is the turn from fear to release, so the verse is built as a list of things she is *giving away*, and the last line flips to what she is *keeping*. The turn lands on line four, which is where the picture cut to her face should sit.
- *Meter.* Lines are roughly iambic heptameter (da-DA × 7), which in 6/8 folk time splits into two singable half-lines. "The NINE-fif-TEEN is PULL-ing OUT" scans clean; the strong syllables fall on strong beats.
- *Prosody on the high note.* Line three lifts to the top note on "quiet house" — "house" is an open vowel that a singer can hold, chosen deliberately over a closed vowel like "kitch" or "shut."
- *Rhyme.* ABAB with perfect rhymes (coat/wrote, wind/behind). Folk register wants closed, clean rhyme, so no slant here. The rhyme words carry meaning — "wrote" (the unsent note, her old silence) and "behind" (the release) both do story work, not filler.
- *Hook / title.* The last half-line, "the year I left behind," is the hook and the likely title. It states the turn without naming the emotion — no "free," no "afraid."
- *Cliché check.* No fire/desire, no heart/apart. "Open door" flirts with cliché but is earned by being literal (she is on a platform, doors are physically open) and by contrast with the "quiet house" she's rejecting.
- *Concreteness.* "The clock I used to wind" carries the whole small-life theme in one object — a clock you wind is old, domestic, repetitive. That single image does what a stanza of "I felt trapped" could not.

## Part B — Score

### How a composer scores to picture

The composer writes music timed to the locked (or near-locked) edit. Work usually runs: **spotting session → sketches / demos → recording → mix**. Cues are written to specific timings, often to a click derived from the picture, so hits land on frame-accurate moments (a door slam, a reveal, a cut).

**Spotting session.** Director, composer, editor, and music supervisor watch the cut together and decide where music starts and stops, what each cue is doing, and where music should deliberately be absent. Silence is a scoring choice. Out of this comes the cue list (M1 for reel 1 cue 1, and so on).

**The composer brief.** What a composer actually needs from a director: the emotional job of each cue (not the genre), reference for tone, what the audience should feel that the picture isn't already saying, and where NOT to score. Talking in adjectives and story ("she doesn't believe him yet") beats talking in instruments. Give the composer the theme's dramatic meaning and let them find the notes.

**Temp tracks and temp love.** Editors cut with temporary music (existing recordings) to test scenes. The danger is **temp love** — director and editor fall for the temp and want the score to copy it, which boxes in the composer and risks legal problems if the temp's feel is too close. Hans Zimmer, Howard Shore, and others have all warned about this. Manage it by spotting to function, not to the temp, and by bringing the composer in early enough to shape taste before temp love sets.

**Leitmotif.** A recurring musical theme tied to a character, place, or idea, transformed as the story transforms it. Wagner's device; in film, John Williams is the reference (the *Star Wars* Force theme, Indiana Jones, the two-note *Jaws* motif). Howard Shore's *Lord of the Rings* score is a full leitmotif system with dozens of interlocking themes. A leitmotif is a writing tool: state the theme plainly early, then bend it (minor key, slower, fragmented) as the character breaks or grows.

**Diegetic vs non-diegetic.** Diegetic = the characters can hear it (a car radio, a bar band, a hummed tune). Non-diegetic = only the audience hears it (the score under a scene). Playing with the boundary is a technique: a song that starts diegetic (a character puts on a record) and swells into non-diegetic score pulls the audience into the character's head. Track which you're in for every music moment.

## Part C — Needle-drops and licensing

### How a music supervisor picks source cues

The music supervisor finds, pitches, and clears existing recordings ("needle-drops") and manages the relationship between picture and rights holders. Selection is dramatic first (does this song do the scene's job, in period, in the character's world?) and practical second (can we afford and clear it?). Study the placement instinct of supervisors like Randall Poster (Scorsese, Wes Anderson), Karyn Rachtman, Thomas Golubić (*Breaking Bad*), and Susan Jacobs (*Big Little Lies*). Scorsese's own use of source music (the "Gimme Shelter" and "Layla" placements) is the touchstone for needle-drop as authorship.

### Two rights, always both

Every recorded song has **two separate copyrights**, and a film use needs both cleared:

- **Sync (synchronization) license** — the right to use the *composition* (the song as written) in timed relation to picture. Cleared with the music publisher(s) / songwriter(s).
- **Master use license** — the right to use the *specific recording*. Cleared with the record label / master owner.

If you clear only one, you cannot use the record. A common cost-saver is clearing the sync and then re-recording the song yourself (a cover / soundalike, which needs its own performers) to avoid the expensive master — but a soundalike made *specifically* to imitate a famous recording is legally risky and has lost in court, so verify before relying on that route.

### Festival vs full clearance

Rights are commonly licensed for a defined **scope**: media (festival only, theatrical, streaming, TV, airline, worldwide), term (a few years vs perpetuity/"in perpetuity"), and territory. A cheap **festival-only** license lets a film screen at festivals but does NOT cover distribution; if the film sells, you must re-clear for full distribution, often at a much higher price and sometimes the holder refuses, forcing a re-edit. Best practice for anything hoping to sell: get a quote for full/worldwide/perpetuity up front, or at least an option, even if you only license festival rights now. This is a live negotiation — verify every scope before relying on it.

### Budget realities (verify before relying — figures drift, as of 2026-07-20)

- Costs are quoted per-side (sync and master quoted separately) and scale with song fame, use (background vs featured vs main title), duration, and scope. A well-known master for broad rights can run into six figures per side; an indie/emerging artist track for festival rights can be a few thousand or less. These ranges move constantly — always get live quotes.
- **Production music / library music** (pre-cleared catalogs like Universal Production Music, APM, Extreme) clears fast and cheap because one licensor controls both sides. Good for background and temp-safe scoring, weaker for signature moments.
- **Commissioned original score** avoids third-party clearance entirely (the production owns or controls the rights it negotiates with the composer) and is often cheaper and safer than a marquee needle-drop.
- Public-domain *compositions* (old enough that the song's copyright has lapsed) still need a master license for any specific modern recording — the recording is separately owned. Verify PD status by territory and date before relying on it.

### Practical order of operations

1. Spot the scene, define the music's job.
2. If a needle-drop, get a rough quote for the scope you actually need *before* cutting the film around it.
3. Keep a music cue sheet from day one (every cue, timing, writers, publishers, masters, shares) — deliverable for distribution and for performance royalties.
4. Have a fallback (a clearable alternative or a commissioned cue) for any song you can't confirm.

- Randall Poster, Thomas Golubić, Susan Jacobs on music supervision; standard sync/master licensing practice — verify all legal and cost specifics with a music lawyer or supervisor before relying.
