# Worked Examples — Before / After

The rubric proven on the page. Each pair: a weak version, the diagnosis, the fixed version. Study these to see what the notes in the base skill *do* to real material. The principle under all of them: cut the on-the-nose, add the subtext, give the scene a turn.

---

## 1. Dialogue — on-the-nose vs. subtext

**Before**
> **SARAH:** I'm so angry at you for forgetting our anniversary. It makes me feel like you don't value our marriage anymore.
> **TOM:** I'm sorry. Work has been stressful and I've been neglecting you. I still love you.

**Diagnosis.** Everyone states their exact feeling and the theme aloud. No subtext, no tactic, nothing for the audience to read. Mamet's law broken: they're delivering information, not pursuing a want. Flat — no turn.

**After**
> Sarah sets one plate on the table. One.
> **SARAH:** There's leftovers. If you're hungry.
> **TOM:** You didn't wait.
> **SARAH:** It's nine o'clock, Tom.
> *(He notices the calendar on the fridge. A date circled in red. Today.)*
> **TOM:** ...Sarah.
> **SARAH:** Don't. (beat) Just eat.

**Why it works.** The anniversary is never named — the circled date and the single plate carry it. Each line is a tactic (she punishes with politeness; he realizes and reaches; she blocks). The behavior tells the audience the marriage is in trouble; the words stay small. The scene *turns* — from cold routine to exposed wound — without a speech.

## 2. Action lines — unfilmable interiority vs. behavior

**Before**
> David remembers the summer his father left, and feels a wave of grief mixed with anger. He knows he has to be strong for his sister, but inside he's falling apart.

**Diagnosis.** None of this is filmable. Memory, "feels," "knows," "inside" — the camera can't shoot a thought. This is novel-writing, not screenwriting.

**After**
> David reads the letter twice. He folds it along the old creases, careful, exact. From the next room, his sister laughs at the TV.
> He puts the letter in his shirt pocket, over the heart. Wipes his face with the flat of his hand. Once.
> **DAVID:** *(calling, steady)* Pizza or noodles?

**Why it works.** Grief and the effort to hold it become *actions* — the careful folding, the single wipe, the steadied voice for his sister's sake. Show, don't tell. An actor can play every line.

## 3. Scene with no turn vs. a scene that turns

**Before** — two cops discuss the case over coffee, recap what the audience already knows, agree to keep investigating, leave. Nothing changes. It's exposition in a costume.

**Diagnosis.** No value shift start-to-end. Cuttable with zero story loss. "Arrive late, leave early" ignored — it's all middle.

**After** — open mid-argument (arrive late): Reyes already thinks the partner is hiding something. Over the scene, a small tell (the partner knows a detail that wasn't released) shifts Reyes from *trusting* to *suspicious* — and she says nothing, just pockets it and changes the subject (leave early, on the new tension). Same information delivered, but now the scene has a **turn** (trust → suspicion) and plants a thread. The recap became a reveal.

## 4. Coverage note — fan reaction vs. professional note

**Weak (fan):** "The second half dragged and I got bored. The ending was kind of unsatisfying."

**Strong (professional):**
> *Structure:* The midpoint (p. 58) is a false victory that costs the hero nothing — he gets the evidence without a sacrifice, so the back half has no new pressure and the pace sags through pp. 60–85.
> *Theme:* The ending resolves the plot (the killer is caught) but not the theme you set up on p. 4 (can he forgive his father?) — the emotional question is dropped, so the win feels hollow.
> *Fix direction:* Make the midpoint cost him the relationship he's been protecting; let the finale force the forgiveness choice, not just the arrest.

**Why it works.** Each note names *where* (page), *what's broken* (the structural cause), and *why* it breaks the experience — and points at a mechanism, not a mood. That's the whole game: diagnose the cause behind the "boring."

## 5. Shot choice — unmotivated vs. motivated

**Before.** "Big slow crane-up and out as they talk, then a drone push through the window. Looks epic."

**Diagnosis.** Movement with no story reason — "epic" is not a motivation. The camera is showing off, not telling.

**After.** Static two-shot while they lie to each other; the moment she says the true thing, a slow push-in on her face isolates her and abandons him in the frame — the move *is* the turn, the camera taking her side as the mask drops. One motivated move beats two spectacular ones. See the image-as-POV law in the base skill.

---

## 6. Logline — generic vs. specific

**Before:** "A man goes on a journey and learns about himself and finds love."

**Diagnosis.** Describes a thousand films. No protagonist specificity, no irony, no stakes, no hook.

**After:** "A grieving cartographer who has never left his village must guide a fugitive across the mountains he's only ever drawn — and choose between the map that made him and the woman who could unmake him." Defining trait (cartographer who never travels), irony (guides across terrain he only mapped), stakes, and a real choice. Only-this-story.

## 7. Exposition delivered *inside* a turn (the hardest, most common problem)

Most real scenes must *transmit plot facts* while still behaving — the marriage beat in #1 has nothing to convey, but 80% of professional scenes do. The amateur move is "As you know, Bob." The pro move is to make the information arrive **as a weapon inside a conflict**, so the audience learns the fact *and* the scene turns on it.

**Before**
> **MILLER:** I can't believe your brother was the mole this whole time. I trusted you and you betrayed the whole department.
> **SARAH:** I'm sorry. I was scared. I didn't know how to tell you.

**Diagnosis.** The reveal ("your brother was the mole") is dumped as flat backstory in line one, everyone announces feelings, and the scene has no turn — both know everything at open and close.

**After**
> **MILLER:** They pulled a name off Reyes's pager. One we never put in a report. Three people knew it.
> *(Sarah keeps pouring coffee after the cup is full. It climbs the rim, spills across the steel. She doesn't stop it.)*
> **MILLER (CONT'D):** Sarah.
> **SARAH:** *(quiet)* He was going to give them my brother.

**Why it works.** The plot fact (Sarah leaked; her brother is the mole) is *never stated* — it's **deduced under pressure**: Miller lays a logic trap ("three people knew it"), and Sarah's overflowing cup confesses before her line does. The exposition arrives as an accusation and a surrender — a **tactic inside conflict**, not a report. The scene *turns* (trust → certainty; Miller's gun rises) on the same information the bad version flatly announced.

**The technique (steal this):** to deliver a required fact without "As you know, Bob" — (1) give one character a **reason to withhold** it and the other a reason to **pry**; (2) let the fact surface as a *move* (an accusation, a slip, a deduction, a thing they can no longer hide), never a briefing; (3) make a **value change on it** so the scene earns its place; (4) **legibility check** — read it cold and confirm the plot beat actually lands (subtext that's so oblique the audience misses the fact is the opposite failure). Cross-ref: `ai-screenwriting-tells.md` ("dialogue as information delivery") and the thriller playbook's "give the audience the bomb" in `genre-playbooks.md`.

---

Pattern across all seven: **the amateur version tells; the professional version behaves** — even when it has hard plot to carry. Strip the stated feeling and the unmotivated flourish, hand the meaning to action, image, and subtext, make required facts arrive as *tactics*, and make sure something *changes*.
