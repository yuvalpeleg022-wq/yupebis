# Genre Playbooks

The general story laws (in the base skill) apply to everything. This file adds what's *specific* to each genre — the craft mechanics a working writer/director actually manipulates, the beats the genre lives or dies on, and the one way each most often fails. Reach for a genre's playbook once you know which contract you've signed (the promise side is in `references/genres-themes.md`).

Each entry: **engine** (what the audience pays for), **must-have beats** (the scene types the form requires), **pro rules** (what a novice botches), **fails as** (signature failure), **exemplars**.

---

## Horror
**Engine.** Dread (anticipation of harm) and shock (its arrival), felt safely. The threat is *impure* — category-violating (Carroll, *The Philosophy of Horror*). The plot is curiosity/disclosure: we tolerate disgust to *know the thing*.
**Must-have beats.** Grounded mundane normal → the rules of the threat established → dread build → **false scare then real scare** (the "cat scare"/"Lewton bus": build to a jolt, resolve it harmlessly, land the real one on the exhale) → the withheld-monster reveal → Final Girl confrontation (Clover, *Men, Women and Chain Saws*).
**Pro rules.** (1) Manage dread as a *resource*, not a switch — the scare is punctuation on a long quiet. (2) **Withhold the monster** — the imagined threat beats the shown one (Jaws' broken shark). (3) Obey your own rules absolutely; a threat that can do anything scares no one. (4) Sound does half the work — silence to prime, stinger to strike; the unseen (acousmatic) source is scarier than the seen.
**Fails as.** A haunted-house ride: all jump scares, no build, audience numb; or the monster shown fully and early, dread collapsed into spectacle.
**Exemplars.** *The Texas Chain Saw Massacre*, *The Shining*, *Alien*, *Hereditary*.

## Thriller / Suspense
**Engine.** Sustained apprehension about an outcome the audience aches to resolve. Threat usually human, plausible, ongoing.
**Must-have beats.** The bomb placed (audience told) → ticking clock → pursuit/near-miss → reversal/rug-pull → false dawn → the bomb goes off.
**Pro rules.** (1) Give the audience **more** information than the characters — Hitchcock's law: surprise is 15 seconds, suspense is 15 minutes and needs the audience to *know* about the bomb (Truffaut, *Hitchcock/Truffaut*). Novices withhold and get confusion, not tension. (2) Objectify the stakes and put a clock on them. (3) Alternate tension and release (the accordion) — unbroken tension flatlines. (4) Protagonist proactive and competent but cornered. (5) Cross-cut to compress time and force the audience to do the arithmetic of proximity.
**Fails as.** Bewilderment (withholding info to fake surprise); or a tease (endless tension, the bomb never goes off).
**Exemplars.** *Rear Window*, *Psycho*, *No Country for Old Men*, *Prisoners*.

## Comedy (and Rom-Com)
**Engine.** Laughter from the safe, pleasurable violation of an expectation — a pattern set and broken (Bergson, *Laughter*: rigidity encrusted on the living).
**Must-have beats.** Setup → punchline; **rule of three** (two to set the pattern, the third breaks it); the comic ramp (escalation/snowball); the topper; running gag with a late callback. **Rom-com:** meet-cute → the "why-don't-they" wall (a real obstacle) → falling-in-love fun-and-games → the dark moment/breakup (the Lie exposed) → the grand gesture.
**Pro rules.** (1) **Timing is structural** — the laugh lives in the order of information and where you cut; funny word last; leave air in the edit to laugh into. (2) Play it straight — characters take the absurd with total seriousness; comedy played *as* comedy dies. (3) Escalate, never repeat flat. (4) The straight man anchors the clown. (5) Specificity: "a duck" beats "a bird." (6) *Rom-com:* the obstacle must be character-based, not a misunderstanding one honest sentence would fix.
**Fails as.** Mugging (playing for comedy), stepping on the laugh; rom-com's third-act misunderstanding that makes the leads look stupid rather than fated.
**Exemplars.** *Some Like It Hot*, *Groundhog Day*, *When Harry Met Sally*, *Bridesmaids*.

## Action
**Engine.** Kinetic problem-solving under physical threat — competence, momentum, consequence in space. Storytelling with the body and the environment.
**Must-have beats.** Set-pieces, each with its own mini-arc (goal/obstacle/escalation/turn/outcome) that *changes the story state*; an escalating gauntlet where each piece differs in *kind* (fist → chase → siege); the reversal inside the fight; the final one-on-one.
**Pro rules.** (1) **Geography first** — always know where everyone is (clean wide before you go tight, hold the 180° line). Fast-cutting to fake energy makes "chaos cinema" — weightless, illegible. (2) Stakes and character ride *inside* the action; the fight dramatizes a relationship. (3) Consequence and cost — injuries persist, ammo runs out, the win costs. (4) Choreograph the reversal (winning → losing → improvise → win). (5) Hold the money shot; fewer, longer, well-lit shots read as *more* dangerous than many fast dark ones.
**Fails as.** Chaos cinema — hyper-cut, disoriented spectacle with no dramatic engine.
**Exemplars.** *Mad Max: Fury Road* (legible geography, centered eyeline), *Die Hard*, *Police Story*, *John Wick*.

## Drama
**Engine.** Emotional truth under pressure — a character forced to choose and changed by the choosing. The base craft at its most exposed; no genre machinery to hide behind.
**Must-have beats.** Inciting disruption → want vs. need split → the dilemma scene (two goods or two evils, never good vs. evil) → the turn + recognition (Aristotle's peripeteia + anagnorisis) → the obligatory confrontation.
**Pro rules.** (1) **Externalize the internal** — dramatize change through action, never announce it. (2) Every scene turns — a value shifts + to − or − to + (McKee, *Story*); a scene where nothing changes is exposition, cut it. (3) Subtext over on-the-nose; the scene is about what's *not* said. (4) The dilemma must be genuine — no obvious right answer. (5) Earn the emotion; underplay, let the audience lean in — manipulation reads as sentimentality.
**Fails as.** On-the-nose emotional exposition and manufactured sentiment; talking-heads scenes with no value change.
**Exemplars.** *Kramer vs. Kramer*, *Manchester by the Sea*, *Tokyo Story*, *Ordinary People*.

## Mystery / Whodunit
**Engine.** Solve a concealed past alongside the detective — the deferred, then earned, *aha*, and the game of "could I have seen it?"
**Must-have beats.** The crime/body → **fair-play clue planting** (every clue needed is shown, disguised) → interrogation rounds (motive/means/opportunity + alibi) → red herring → false solution → the denouement where the clues retroactively click.
**Pro rules.** (1) **Plant fairly, pay off surprisingly** — bury the key clue in a cluster of vivid nothings, or under high emotion, so it files as color; the solution is *surprising yet inevitable*. (2) The killer present and viable early (Knox's Decalogue; Van Dine's Twenty Rules — no last-minute stranger). (3) Control who knows what when — withhold the detective's *interpretation*, never the *evidence*. (4) Every red herring needs a satisfying innocent explanation. (5) The solution recontextualizes rather than adds — the audience re-sees earlier scenes.
**Fails as.** The unfair mystery (the clue was never available — reveal feels arbitrary); or telegraphing (audience 40 minutes ahead of the detective).
**Exemplars.** *Knives Out*, *Chinatown*, *Zodiac*.

## Sci-Fi
**Engine.** Extrapolation — "what if?" pushed to consequence. One *novum* treated with rigorous logic (Suvin's cognitive estrangement). The real subject is the present, defamiliarized.
**Must-have beats.** Premise/world-rules established → sense-of-wonder reveal (scale) → iceberg worldbuilding drip → the consequence spiral where the premise's logic drives the ending.
**Pro rules.** (1) **One big lie, then relentless internal consistency** — the audience forgives one impossible thing if everything else follows. (2) Dramatize the idea through character, never lecture — bury worldbuilding in conflict, no "As you know, Bob." (3) **Iceberg worldbuilding** — show 10%, imply 90%; the "used future" (lived-in, dirty). (4) Ground the extraordinary in a human-scale throughline (love, grief). (5) Extrapolate honestly, past the easy answer.
**Fails as.** Exposition dumps and concept-over-character (a fascinating world nobody cares about); internal-logic cheats that dissolve stakes.
**Exemplars.** *Blade Runner*, *Children of Men*, *Arrival*, *Ex Machina*.

## Fantasy
**Engine.** Immersion in a secondary world with consistent rules; wonder and the eucatastrophe — the sudden joyous turn (Tolkien, "On Fairy-Stories").
**Must-have beats.** Ordinary world → call/threshold crossing → mentor + the gift (rules + tool) → tests/allies/enemies → wonder-reveal → the against-all-odds turn to joy.
**Pro rules.** (1) **Sanderson's First Law** — magic that *solves* problems must be understood by the audience (hard magic); magic for wonder/mystery must *not* resolve the plot (soft magic — Gandalf). Undefined magic solving corners is deus ex machina. (2) **Sanderson's Second Law** — limitations and costs beat powers. (3) Consistency and implied depth (Tolkien's languages/history sell a real past). (4) Ground the epic in the small (Frodo's feet, Sam's loyalty). (5) Stakes moral, not just martial — the world externalizes an inner struggle.
**Fails as.** Magic-as-plaster (convenient undefined powers) and worldbuilding-as-homework (lore dumped, not dramatized).
**Exemplars.** *The Lord of the Rings*, *Pan's Labyrinth*, *Spirited Away*.

## Crime / Gangster
**Engine.** The rise-and-fall tragedy of ambition outside the law — the American Dream's dark mirror. We're seduced by the transgressor's appetite, then made to watch the cost (Warshow, "The Gangster as Tragic Hero").
**Must-have beats.** The come-up (ascent through a code) → escalating violence → the crossing-a-line beat → hubris/peak → the fall (betrayal, the walls close). The code/loyalty tests recur.
**Pro rules.** (1) **Seduce, then indict** — make the life *fun* (Goodfellas' two hours of glamour) so the fall hurts; moralize from the start and it's boring, glamorize without cost and it's hollow. (2) World before plot — the milieu (rituals, argot, economics) is the star. (3) The code is the tragedy engine — the gangster falls by breaking his own world's rules. (4) Violence sudden, ugly, costly — not choreographed cool. (5) The protagonist is agent of his own doom (hubris, not bad luck).
**Fails as.** Glamorization without reckoning (a music video that endorses the life), or moralizing so heavily the appeal is never felt.
**Exemplars.** *The Godfather*, *Goodfellas*, *Scarface*.

## War
**Engine.** The individual under the maximum pressure of organized violence — what a person is when everything is stripped away.
**Must-have beats.** Unit/ensemble intro (platoon as microcosm) → the making of a soldier → mission/objective → the ambush and fog of war → the combat set-piece → the moral-injury beat → the impossible return.
**Pro rules.** (1) **The individual over the map** — soldier's-eye level; feel the confusion, don't read the strategy. (2) Chaos by design but emotionally legible (Spielberg's shutter-speed handheld on Omaha Beach) — always track your person's feeling even when geography fractures. (3) Earn the anti-war stance through experience, not sermon — combat horrifying and consequential, not thrilling (Truffaut's trap: spectacle betrays the message). (4) Brotherhood is the emotional engine — differentiate soldiers so deaths cost. (5) Moral ambiguity over jingoism.
**Fails as.** Combat that thrills more than it horrifies (accidental glory); interchangeable soldiers whose deaths carry no weight.
**Exemplars.** *Saving Private Ryan*, *Come and See*, *Platoon*, *Full Metal Jacket*.

## Western
**Engine.** Civilization vs. wilderness at the frontier — the lone man who brings order but has no place in the order he creates; the passing of a way of life.
**Must-have beats.** The stranger rides in → frontier-vs.-town tension → the ritualized showdown/duel → the hero who must leave or die.
**Pro rules.** (1) **Landscape is a character and a moral force** — tiny figures in vast frames *is* the meaning (Ford's Monument Valley). (2) Restraint before violence — the showdown works because of the long stillness before it (Leone's held silence + close-ups + Morricone). (3) The hero is obsolete by design — he civilizes the frontier and is made unnecessary (Shane). (4) Myth vs. reality — "print the legend"; the revisionist Western interrogates the violence the classic one romanticized (*Unforgiven*). (5) Economy and silence — trust image and gesture.
**Fails as.** Western-as-costume (tropes without mythic weight or landscape-as-theme); rushing the ritual so the showdown has no charge.
**Exemplars.** *The Searchers*, *Once Upon a Time in the West*, *Unforgiven*.

## Musical
**Engine.** Emotion so large it can only be sung/danced — the number *is* the storytelling; the pleasure is release into stylization.
**Must-have beats.** The **"I Want" song** (early — the yearning that drives the story) → the conditional love song → the production number → the reprise (a motif returns transformed) → the eleven o'clock showstopper.
**Pro rules.** (1) **Every number must move the story** — the character enters in one emotional state and exits another; a song that changes nothing halts the film. (2) Motivate the shift into song — build pressure until singing is the only release. (3) Choreograph for the camera: full body in frame, hold the shot (Astaire's rule); chop-cutting fakes dancing the leads can't do. (4) Pick your diegetic convention (do people literally sing, or is it metaphor?) and hold it. (5) Reprise and motif are your structural rhyme.
**Fails as.** Plot-neutral numbers that stop the story; hyper-cut choreography hiding that no one can dance.
**Exemplars.** *Singin' in the Rain*, *West Side Story*, *La La Land*.

## Coming-of-Age
**Engine.** Loss of innocence — a one-way threshold from one understanding to a harder one. The engine is *irreversible internal change*.
**Must-have beats.** Naïve equilibrium → the disruptive first (love, death, betrayal, injustice) → the mentor/false idol revealed as fallible → the threshold event (point of no return) → reintegration, the world re-seen.
**Pro rules.** (1) The change is **irreversible and specific** — pinned to a concrete event and a concrete new understanding, not "they grew up." (2) The world doesn't change — the *perception* does; the craft move is the POV shift (the parent re-seen with adult eyes). (3) Small stakes felt hugely — honor a first heartbreak from inside the character; don't inflate or condescend. (4) Nostalgia is texture, not crutch — the emotion stays present-tense. (5) Resist the neat lesson — growth is also loss.
**Fails as.** The sanitized lesson-shaped arc where growing up is stated not experienced; adult condescension toward the kid's stakes.
**Exemplars.** *Stand By Me*, *Lady Bird*, *The 400 Blows*, *Moonlight*.

## Romance
**Engine.** Two people whose union is both desired and obstructed. Unlike rom-com it can end in tragedy; the engine is *yearning* against obstacle.
**Must-have beats.** The meeting/spark → the obstacle (the "no": fear, class, duty, war, death) → intimacy escalation (vulnerability exchanged) → the crisis/separation → union (comedy) or loss (tragedy) — the feeling delivered either way.
**Pro rules.** (1) **Chemistry is written, not cast** — built from want, banter, complementary need; attractive people staring isn't chemistry. (2) The obstacle must be load-bearing and ideally internal/thematic, so the love story is also a change story. (3) **Withhold the union** — delay is the pleasure; the near-miss, the almost-kiss; consummate too early and the engine dies. (4) Intimacy through vulnerability and specific gesture, not physical alone. (5) Both characters get an arc and agency — no love-object-as-prize.
**Fails as.** Insta-love with no obstacle or yearning; a passive love-object with no interiority.
**Exemplars.** *Casablanca*, *In the Mood for Love*, *Brokeback Mountain*, *Before Sunrise*.

## Noir / Neo-Noir
**Engine.** A morally compromised protagonist pulled into a corrupt world and destroyed (or barely surviving) by his own weakness — usually desire. As much worldview (fatalism) as plot. The engine is *doom* — already lost.
**Must-have beats.** The hook/the dame walks in → the femme fatale → deepening entanglement past the point of return → the double-cross/twist → the fatalistic ending → often voiceover/flashback (the doomed man narrating his end).
**Pro rules.** (1) **Style is content** — chiaroscuro, venetian-blind slats, wet night streets, Dutch angles externalize the moral darkness (Schrader, "Notes on Film Noir": noir is a tone, defined by style). (2) The protagonist is complicit, not heroic — the fall is his own doing, character-deep, not lighting pasted on a clean hero. (3) The femme fatale has agency and her own agenda — not a prop. (4) Fatalism and moral ambiguity — institutions corrupt top to bottom, no clean win. (5) Hardboiled voice and a labyrinth plot where the *mood of paranoia* outranks every plot mechanic tracking.
**Fails as.** Noir cosplay — shadows, fedoras, voiceover over a clean hero and a happy ending.
**Exemplars.** *Double Indemnity*, *Chinatown*, *Blade Runner*, *Blood Simple*.

## Documentary
**Engine.** Structured actuality — shaping real footage into a narrative or argument that reveals truth. The craft tension: fidelity to the real vs. the shaping that makes it mean.
**Must-have beats (mode-dependent).** Choose among **Nichols' six modes** — Expository (voice-of-God), Observational (fly-on-the-wall), Participatory (the filmmaker interacts), Reflexive (the film examines itself), Poetic (mood/association), Performative (subjective). Building blocks: the interview, B-roll/archival, the vérité scene, the reveal/turning point — a three-act arc *imposed* on real material.
**Pro rules.** (1) **Structure is built in the EDIT** — you find the narrative in the footage; shoot for coverage, then sculpt the arc. (2) Characters and stakes even in nonfiction — find your protagonist, their want, what's at risk. (3) Show, don't narrate — vérité over wall-to-wall VO (over-reliance on voiceover is the amateur tell, often a symptom of thin footage). (4) Ethics and access are craft — consent, the responsibility of the frame; misleading juxtaposition/"Frankenbiting" betrays the audience. (5) POV is inevitable — own an honest one; every cut is an argument.
**Fails as.** The info-dump lecture — wall-to-wall narration over B-roll, no character, no stakes, no arc; or dishonest editing that manufactures a false reality.
**Exemplars.** *Hoop Dreams*, *The Thin Blue Line*, *Grey Gardens*.

## Biopic
**Engine.** Illuminate a real life by finding the *dramatic* truth inside the factual one — a life with a defining tension (genius vs. flaw, public vs. private self).
**Must-have beats.** The defining early moment → the rise → the flaw/the wound (the private cost — the "Rosebud") → the fall/crisis → reckoning or transcendence, with recreation set-pieces (the famous speech/performance/match).
**Pro rules.** (1) **Dramatize a theme, not a Wikipedia timeline** — the fatal mistake is cradle-to-grave chronology; narrow the window (*Lincoln* = one month, *Steve Jobs* = three launches). Constraint creates drama. (2) Compress and invent within the truth (composites, condensed timelines) — fidelity to emotional/thematic truth, but never invert the meaning of a real life. (3) Find the flaw — hagiography is dramatically dead; live in the gap between public myth and private person. (4) Enter late, leave early — start when the defining tension ignites, not at birth. (5) Avoid the greatest-hits trap — each recreated beat must carry story weight, not just recognition.
**Fails as.** The cradle-to-grave greatest-hits reel — reverent, shapeless, no theme, no flaw, no dramatic question.
**Exemplars.** *Raging Bull*, *The Social Network*, *Lincoln*, *Amadeus*.

## Heist
**Engine.** The pleasure of a plan — assemble a team and a scheme, then watch it collide with reality. Admire competence, delight in cleverness. A precision puzzle box; the most formulaic genre, and that's the pleasure.
**Must-have beats.** The score proposed (the impossible target) → assembling the crew (each specialist introduced by skill) → casing/the plan (shown to the audience — a version of it) → seeded obstacles → the execution and apparent disaster → **the twist** (a flashback reveals the plan we saw was incomplete) → the getaway/double-cross.
**Pro rules.** (1) **Plant everything; the twist must be fair** — the reveal uses elements the audience *saw* but misread (the same double-clue technique as Mystery, in reverse). Cheating the twist with information never shown cons the audience, not the mark. (2) Establish the rules and obstacles precisely — the vault, the security, the timing window — so the execution is legible. (3) Show the plan, then break it — the gap between rehearsal and chaos is the suspense. (4) Every specialty must pay off (a Chekhov's gun for skills). (5) Character wants ride inside the caper — loyalty, betrayal, the last job.
**Fails as.** The unfair twist (built on info the audience never had) and the cold caper with no character stakes.
**Exemplars.** *Ocean's Eleven*, *The Sting*, *Rififi*, *Inception* (heist grammar on dreams).

## Cross-genre anchors
These recur across genres — learn them once, apply everywhere:
- **Suspense vs. surprise** (Hitchcock/Truffaut) — Thriller, Horror, Heist.
- **The fair-play plant-and-payoff / double clue** — Mystery, Heist, any twist.
- **The scene must turn** (McKee) — Drama and every genre's scene work.
- **Internal-logic consistency + iceberg worldbuilding** — Sci-Fi, Fantasy.
- **Seduce then indict / earn the theme through experience** — Crime, War, Noir.
- **Legible geography vs. chaos cinema** (Bordwell, "intensified continuity") — Action, Musical, any staged physical set-piece.
- **Structure is found in the edit** — Documentary most nakedly, but true of all film.
