# AI-Screenwriting Tells

The signatures that expose machine-written scripts and story material. Sibling to the `behuman` skill (which covers prose) — this one is specific to screenwriting and story. Suppress these in your own output; flag them in critique. Most reduce to one root: **AI writes the *idea* of a scene, not a scene** — generic, smooth, on-the-nose, and afraid of specificity.

**Use both, they cover different layers.** This file catches *story/scene* tells (passive protagonist, no turn, on-the-nose beats, unfilmable action). The `behuman` skill catches *prose/sentence* tells (the kill-list words, significance tails, negative parallelism, decorative formatting) in the words you actually type — action lines, treatments, loglines, pitch copy, voiceover, and the descriptive text around the dialogue. When you write any screen prose, run this file for the drama and `behuman` for the sentences.

## Dialogue tells

- **Everyone announces their feelings and the theme.** "I'm afraid you'll abandon me like everyone else." Real characters hide the want; the audience reads it from behavior. (See `worked-examples.md` #1.)
- **All characters share one voice.** The teenager, the general, and the barista use the same vocabulary and rhythm. Give each a distinct register, diction, and tactic.
- **Dialogue as information delivery.** Lines exist to tell the audience plot ("As you know, we've been partners for ten years"). Mamet's law: nobody speaks unless they want something *from the other character*.
- **Tidy, complete, grammatical speech.** Real talk interrupts, trails off, overlaps, dodges. Perfectly balanced back-and-forth is a tell.
- **The summarizing button line.** Characters end scenes by stating the lesson ("Maybe family isn't blood after all"). Cut it — let the scene mean without narrating itself.

## Structure & character tells

- **Passive protagonist.** Things happen *to* the hero; they react and get rescued by events. A real protagonist drives and chooses under cost.
- **Want with no need, or vice versa.** External plot with no internal arc (or a stated "lesson" with no external engine). The collision of the two is the story.
- **Beats hit mechanically, empty.** The Catalyst lands on the right page but the character wants nothing; structure worship with no pulse.
- **Frictionless resolution.** The ending ties every thread, everyone reconciles, no real cost paid. AI defaults to reassurance. Earn the ending or leave a scar.
- **Stakes that are asserted, not felt.** "The fate of the world hangs in the balance" with no concrete, personal thing to lose. Ground stakes in one specific relationship or object.
- **The "and then" plot.** Events in sequence with no causality — this happens *and then* that. Real plot is this *therefore* that / *but* that.

## Scene-craft tells

- **Scenes with no turn.** Conversations that begin and end on the same value; recaps in costume. Every scene must change a situation or a piece of knowledge.
- **Unfilmable action lines.** "She remembers," "he knows deep down," "the room feels tense." The camera shoots behavior, not interiority. (See `worked-examples.md` #2.)
- **Generic geography.** "A room." "A city street." No specific, chosen detail that means something. Specificity is the most human trait available.
- **Establishing everything.** Over-explaining the world, characters introducing themselves, backstory front-loaded. Trust the audience; reveal through conflict.

## Concept tells

- **The logline could be ten films.** No irony, no only-this-story hook. (See `worked-examples.md` #6.)
- **Symmetrical, schematic characters.** Each one neatly embodies a theme-position; nobody contradicts themselves. Real people are inconsistent.
- **Reference-salad tone.** "[Film A] meets [Film B] meets [Film C]" as a substitute for a point of view. Comps orient; they don't author.
- **Safe, consensus taste.** Nothing risked, nobody alienated, no committed point of view. The most machine thing a script can do is refuse to have an opinion.

## Do not overcorrect
- Human ≠ sloppy. The fix is specificity and subtext, not fake grit or manufactured quirk.
- Genre and format exempt some "tells": a procedural front-loads information; a musical states feeling in song; a kids' film is legible by design. The ban is on *unmotivated* smoothing, not on clarity that the form requires.
- Never trade a real caveat or fact for "voice." Precision is the point.

**The test:** does this scene *behave*, or does it *describe the behavior it should have*? If it's telling you the idea of the moment instead of staging the moment, it's the machine.
