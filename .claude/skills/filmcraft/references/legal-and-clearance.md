# Legal and clearance

**THIS IS NOT LEGAL ADVICE.** This is general production information to help you understand the moving parts and ask better questions. It is not a substitute for a qualified entertainment attorney, and reading it does not create any professional relationship. Every real decision about rights, contracts, clearance, and insurance requires a licensed entertainment attorney in the relevant jurisdiction. Laws differ by country, state, and emirate; what follows describes common US/UK-industry practice and may not apply where you are. Today is 2026-07-20; statutes, case law, and standard terms change — do not rely on anything here without current professional confirmation.

Use this document to prepare, not to decide. When a section says "confirm with counsel," that is the operative instruction.

## Chain of title

Chain of title is the unbroken paper trail proving who owns the rights to the material, from the original source through every option, assignment, and agreement, to the production entity. A distributor, financier, and E&O insurer will all demand it before money moves.

- **Source material:** if the film is based on a book, article, life story, prior film, or other IP, you need documented rights from the actual owner. Public-domain status is a legal conclusion, not an assumption — confirm it with counsel, because a work can be public domain in one country and protected in another.
- **Writer agreements:** every writer who touched the script must have a signed agreement assigning their contribution to the production entity. An uncredited rewrite with no paperwork is a hole in the chain.
- **Assignments:** rights must be affirmatively transferred in writing to the entity that will license the film. Verbal deals and handshake credits do not hold.
- **A break anywhere voids the whole chain.** One missing signature can make the film unsalable. This is not legal advice — it is a reason to involve counsel from the first option.

## Option and purchase agreements

- **Option agreement:** you pay for the exclusive right, for a limited period, to buy the underlying rights later at a pre-agreed price. Options let a producer control material without buying it outright while assembling a package. Terms — option period, extension fees, the eventual purchase price, and the scope of rights (film, TV, sequel, remake, merchandising, festival) — are all negotiable and all consequential.
- **Purchase / assignment agreement:** the actual transfer of rights, triggered when the option is exercised. Defines exactly what the buyer gets and what the seller keeps (reserved rights such as publishing, stage, or author-written sequels).
- **Reserved rights and "frozen rights"** clauses, turnaround provisions, and reversion (rights returning to the author if the film is not made within a window) are where deals get complicated. Confirm every one with an entertainment attorney. This is not legal advice.

## Rights and clearances

Clearance is the process of confirming you have the right to use everything visible or audible in the film. Each category has its own regime.

- **Script clearance:** a clearance report (often from a professional clearance service) flags real names, businesses, brands, phone numbers, addresses, and potentially defamatory or infringing content. Fictional names get checked against real people; a coincidental match with a real person in the same city and profession is a risk. Counsel and the E&O insurer drive what must change.
- **Music:** two separate rights for a recorded song — the **master use** license (the specific recording, held by the label) and the **synchronization (sync)** license (the underlying composition, held by the publisher). You need both to use a track. Original score is commissioned under a composer agreement (work-for-hire, with performer/musician clearances). "It's just background" is not a defense. Confirm all music with counsel and a music supervisor.
- **Trademarks and brands:** visible logos, product packaging, and branded goods may need clearance or set dressing changes. Incidental background use versus prominent or unflattering use is a legal distinction, not a producer's judgment call. Confirm with counsel.
- **Locations:** the right to film at a location comes from a location agreement with the property owner, plus any required permits (below). Recognizable private property, distinctive architecture, and some public landmarks can carry restrictions.
- **Depiction / likeness / life rights:** portraying a real, identifiable living person raises rights of publicity, privacy, and defamation exposure. "Life rights" agreements are often obtained not because a person's life story is itself copyrightable but to secure cooperation, releases, and a covenant not to sue. Depicting the dead, public figures, and private individuals each carry different risk. This is squarely attorney territory — confirm with counsel before writing a real person into the film.

## Fair use (basics only — confirm with counsel)

Fair use (US) is a **defense**, decided case by case, not a checkbox you can clear yourself. US courts weigh four factors: the purpose and character of the use (including whether it is transformative and whether it is commercial), the nature of the copyrighted work, the amount and substantiality used, and the effect on the market for the original. Other countries use different doctrines (e.g., "fair dealing"), which are narrower. Relying on fair use without a lawyer's written opinion is how films get pulled from distribution. E&O insurers frequently require a fair-use opinion letter from counsel before they will cover a use. **This is not legal advice — treat any fair-use question as a stop-and-call-counsel moment.**

## Work-for-hire and credits (confirm with counsel)

- **Work-for-hire:** the mechanism by which a hiring entity, not the individual creator, is treated as the author/owner of contributed work (a score, a rewrite, design work). It has specific statutory requirements and does not apply automatically to every arrangement — a written agreement is usually required, and the details matter. Getting this wrong leaves ownership with the individual and puts a hole in the chain of title.
- **Credit obligations:** contracts specify credits — on-screen position, size, billing order, paid-ad credit. Guild agreements impose their own credit rules (the WGA determines writing credit through a formal process). Credit is a contractual and sometimes legal obligation, not a courtesy, and breaching it can trigger claims. Marketing materials (posters, trailers) must honor the billing block. Confirm all credit terms with counsel.

## Title clearance (confirm with counsel)

Before a title is locked, it is checked for conflicts — other films with the same or a confusingly similar title, trademark issues, and (in the US industry) the MPA's title registration system among signatory studios. A title can also raise its own trademark or right-of-publicity questions. Title clearance is a distinct step from script clearance and is typically part of the E&O and legal review. Confirm with counsel and, where applicable, the title registration body. This is not legal advice.

## Errors and omissions (E&O) insurance

E&O insurance protects the production and distributor against claims of infringement, defamation, invasion of privacy, and rights violations. Distributors almost always require it as a delivery item. The insurer conducts its own review and will not issue a policy until clearance is satisfactory — a clean script clearance report, signed releases, proven chain of title, and often counsel opinion letters for anything borderline. E&O is where all the clearance work gets tested. Coverage terms, limits, and requirements vary by carrier and change — confirm current requirements with your broker and counsel.

## Permits

- Filming on public property, streets, or in many private venues requires a permit from the relevant film office or authority, plus proof of liability insurance. Requirements differ by jurisdiction — a city, a national film commission, or (where you are) the relevant emirate's media authority each have their own process.
- Drones, stunts, pyrotechnics, weapons, animals, and minors on set trigger additional permits, licensed supervisors, and safety regulations. These are legal and safety obligations, not formalities.
- Confirm every permit requirement with the local film office and counsel before shooting. Shooting without required permits can void insurance and expose the production to liability.

## Releases

A release is a signed grant of permission. Get them in writing, before or at the moment of filming — chasing signatures after the shoot is where films lose footage.

- **Talent / performer release:** grants the right to record and use a performer's image, voice, and performance. For union productions, guild agreements (SAG-AFTRA and equivalents) govern much of this — confirm applicability with counsel.
- **Location release:** the property owner's grant to film and to use the footage.
- **Appearance / personal release:** for anyone recognizable on camera who is not cast — crowd, background, real people in documentaries. Documentaries live and die on these.
- **Materials / artwork release:** for visible copyrighted artwork, photographs, signage, or other creative works.
- **Minors:** additional consent from parents/guardians, plus jurisdiction-specific labor rules, work-hour limits, and on-set supervision. Confirm with counsel.

## Archival, stock, and generated material (confirm with counsel)

- **Stock footage and archival:** licensed from a library or rights holder under specific terms (media, territory, duration). "Found on the internet" is not licensed. News and archival clips can carry multiple layers of rights (the footage, the music under it, people depicted in it) — each must clear.
- **Photographs and artwork on screen:** a photograph is a copyrighted work; showing it prominently needs the photographer's or rights holder's permission, and if it depicts an identifiable person, potentially theirs too.
- **User-generated and social media content:** a tweet, post, or clip is authored by someone who holds rights in it. Reproducing it on screen is a use that needs clearance, regardless of it being "public."
- **AI-generated material:** the copyright status and clearance exposure of AI-generated images, audio, and footage is unsettled and moving. Whether such material can be protected, whether it infringes its training sources, and whether a distributor's E&O carrier will cover it are open questions that must go to counsel. Do not assume generated content is clearance-free. This is not legal advice.

## Defamation, privacy, and publicity (confirm with counsel)

Separate from copyright, portraying real people or events exposes a production to defamation (a false statement of fact that harms reputation), invasion of privacy (disclosure of private facts, false light), and right of publicity (unauthorized commercial use of a person's identity) claims. The standards differ for public figures versus private individuals, for the living versus the dead, and by jurisdiction. "Based on a true story" and fictionalized composites do not automatically insulate a production — a fictional character recognizable as a real person can still ground a claim. This is core attorney territory; every real-person depiction needs legal review before it is shot. This is not legal advice.

## Guilds, unions, and residuals (confirm with counsel)

If a production is signatory to a guild (SAG-AFTRA for performers, WGA for writers, DGA for directors, IATSE for crew, or their equivalents in your country), the relevant collective bargaining agreement governs minimums, working conditions, credits, and residuals — payments owed to talent and crew from downstream revenue (streaming, TV, home video). Residual obligations follow the film into distribution and are a real, ongoing liability that a distributor and financier will account for. Whether and how a guild agreement applies, and what it costs over the life of the film, is a question for counsel and a line producer, not an assumption. This is not legal advice.

## International co-production (confirm with counsel)

Financing a film across countries — using co-production treaties, tax credits, rebates, and "soft money" — brings each jurisdiction's own rules on ownership, nationality qualification, spend requirements, and rights. A treaty co-production can unlock funding and market access but binds the film to eligibility conditions (local spend, local cast/crew, points systems) that shape the whole production. Chain of title and clearance must satisfy every territory involved, and public-domain and fair-use conclusions do not travel across borders. Every cross-border structure requires counsel in each jurisdiction. This is not legal advice.

## Clearance checklist

A preparation aid, not a legal sign-off. A qualified entertainment attorney must review the actual film. Confirm every item with counsel and your E&O insurer.

- [ ] Chain of title complete and documented (source rights, all writer agreements, all assignments).
- [ ] Option/purchase agreement executed; reserved rights and reversion terms understood.
- [ ] Professional script clearance report obtained and every flag resolved.
- [ ] All music cleared — master use **and** sync for every recorded track; composer and musician agreements for score.
- [ ] Trademarks, logos, and branded products cleared or removed/altered.
- [ ] Location agreements signed for every location; all permits obtained.
- [ ] Depiction/likeness/life-rights issues for any real person reviewed by counsel.
- [ ] Any fair-use reliance backed by a written opinion letter from counsel.
- [ ] Talent, location, appearance, materials, and minors releases collected and filed.
- [ ] E&O insurance bound, with all insurer requirements satisfied.
- [ ] Entertainment attorney has reviewed the complete package before delivery.

## When to bring in counsel (a timeline)

Legal review is cheapest early and most expensive after the film is shot. Bring in a qualified entertainment attorney at these points — earlier if anything is unusual:

- **Before optioning or buying any material** — to structure the option/purchase and start the chain of title clean.
- **Before writing a real person or real event into the script** — depiction, defamation, and life-rights exposure is far easier to design around on the page than to fix in the edit.
- **During script development** — to run a script clearance report and flag names, brands, music intentions, and real-world references while they can still be changed cheaply.
- **Before the shoot** — to confirm permits, releases, location agreements, and union/guild status, and to finalize what must clear.
- **In post and before delivery** — to close music licenses, resolve every clearance flag, secure E&O, and assemble the delivery/chain-of-title package a distributor requires.

Skipping the early check to save money is the single most common way independent films end up with an unsalable rights problem discovered at the worst possible moment. This is not legal advice — it is a reason to call counsel sooner rather than later.

**Reminder, one more time: none of this is legal advice.** It is a map of the terrain so you know which questions to bring to a qualified entertainment attorney, who is required for any real decision. Laws and standard terms vary by jurisdiction and change over time. When in doubt, stop and call counsel.
