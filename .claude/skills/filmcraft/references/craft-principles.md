# The Craft of Filmmaking — A Practitioner Reference

Structure per discipline: **PRINCIPLE** (the working idea), **VOCABULARY** (the terms pros actually use on set / in the room), **AMATEUR MISTAKE** (the tell). Grounded in the sources at the end.

---

## 1. Story & Screenwriting

### Core principle
Story is not events; it is a **character pursuing a goal against escalating resistance, forced to change**. Structure exists to manage that pressure and the reveal of change. Every structural model below is a different container for the same engine: desire, obstacle, decision, cost, transformation.

### Structure models (know all; they rhyme)
- **Three-act structure** — Setup / Confrontation / Resolution, hinged on two turning points ("plot points"). Act 2 is where most scripts collapse because the container is loosest there.
- **Save the Cat! (Blake Snyder) — 15 beats** in order: Opening Image → Theme Stated → Set-Up → Catalyst → Debate → Break into Two → B Story → Fun and Games (the "promise of the premise") → Midpoint (false victory/defeat, stakes raised) → Bad Guys Close In → All Is Lost (often a "whiff of death") → Dark Night of the Soul → Break into Three → Finale → Final Image. Snyder assigns **page/percentage positions** against a 110-page script; use the percentage column for any other length. Its value is specifically **taming Act 2** via the Midpoint and "Bad Guys Close In."
- **Sequence method** — the feature as **8 sequences** (~10–15 min each), each a mini-movie with its own tension question and mini-climax. Prevents the "saggy middle" by giving Act 2 four smaller dramatic units instead of one 60-minute void. Roots in silent-era reel structure.
- **Dan Harmon Story Circle — 8 steps**: You (comfort zone) → Need (want something) → Go (enter an unfamiliar situation) → Search (adapt) → Find (get what they wanted) → Take (pay a heavy price) → Return (back to the familiar) → Change (transformed). A repeatable Hero's Journey; top half order, bottom half chaos.
- **Kishōtenketsu** — East Asian **4-act** form: Ki (introduction) → Shō (development) → **Ten (the twist / unexpected turn)** → Ketsu (reconciliation). Generates meaning **without central conflict** — the "ten" recontextualizes what came before rather than opposing it. Use for quiet, tonal, mood pieces that Western conflict models would deform.

### Scene construction
- A scene is a **unit of conflict with a turn**: it must change a character's situation or knowledge from beginning to end (value shift, e.g. + to −). No turn = the scene is exposition wearing a costume.
- **"Arrive late, leave early"** — enter on the latest possible moment, cut before the energy dies.
- **The "what's this scene really about?" test** — every scene must answer: *whose scene is it* (whose objective drives it) and *what changes*. If you can cut it and the story survives intact, it isn't earning its place.

### Character: want vs. need
- **Want** = the conscious, external goal (the plot engine). **Need** = the unconscious, internal lesson required to become whole (the theme engine). The arc is the collision: usually the character must **abandon the want to satisfy the need** (or gets the want and learns it was hollow — the "Take" beat).
- **Objective / super-objective** (Stanislavski): what a character pursues in a scene vs. across the whole story.

### Subtext, dialogue, show-don't-tell
- **Subtext** = the real meaning underneath the spoken line — "externalizing the internal." Build it by writing each character's want, then **giving each a reason they can't say it directly**. The gap between said and meant is the subtext.
- **Mamet's law of dialogue**: *nobody says anything unless they want something.* Every line is a tactic to get a scene-objective, not information delivery.
- **Show-don't-tell** = reveal emotion/conflict/story through **behavior, action, and visual detail**, not stated explanation. If the words stay polite while the behavior turns cold, the audience reads the real tension. On-the-nose dialogue is the opposite: characters announcing their feelings and the theme aloud.
- **Theme stated (not preached)** — a well-built script plants the thematic question early (often in someone else's mouth, and the hero dismisses it), then dramatizes the answer through choices, never a speech.

### Common amateur mistakes
- **Passive protagonist** — things happen *to* the hero; they don't drive.
- **On-the-nose dialogue** — no subtext; characters say exactly what they feel and what the movie is "about."
- **Exposition dumps** ("As you know, Bob…") instead of dramatizing information through conflict.
- **No want/need distinction** — external plot with no internal arc, so nothing is at stake emotionally.
- **Scenes with no turn** — flat conversations that begin and end on the same emotional value.
- **Structure worship** — hitting beats mechanically while ignoring whether the character actually wants anything. Beats are diagnostics, not paint-by-numbers.
- **Undramatized theme** — stating the message rather than proving it through the ending's cost.

## 2. Cinematography

### Core principle
An image feels **intentional** when every choice — size, lens, height, movement, light — is **motivated by story and point of view**, not by convenience. The camera is a character with an attitude; where it stands and what it can see *is* the storytelling.

### Shot sizes (grammar of proximity → emotion)
Extreme Wide (EWS/establishing) → Wide/Long (WS) → Medium Wide → Medium (MS) → Medium Close-Up (MCU) → Close-Up (CU) → Extreme Close-Up (ECU). Rule of thumb: **wider = context and vulnerability/isolation; tighter = intimacy and intensity.** Save the tightest sizes for the moments that earn them (escalation, not default).

### Lens language & compression
- **Focal length** governs both field of view and the *relationship between planes*.
- **Wide lens** (short focal length, ~18–35mm): expansive field, **exaggerated depth**, foreground looms large, background pushed away and small; minimizes visible shake; distorts faces up close. Isolates a subject by *opening up* space around them.
- **Long / telephoto lens** (~85–200mm+): **compresses** space — background appears pulled forward and stacked onto the subject; flattering faces; **shallow depth of field** isolates subject via focus. Isolates by *compressing* space onto them.
- **Storytelling use**: some films commit to one end (Kubrick's wides, telephoto vérité); some **gradually shift focal length across a film** to track a character's psychological change. Lens is a POV statement, not just a framing tool.

### Camera movement & motivation
- Types: **pan / tilt** (pivot on axis), **dolly / track** (whole camera translates — reveals depth via parallax), **crane / jib**, **Steadicam / gimbal** (floating), **handheld** (subjective, unstable, urgent), **zoom** (optical magnification — flattens; feels different from a dolly because planes don't shift). **Dolly ≠ zoom.**
- **Motivated movement**: the camera moves *because the story does* — following action, revealing information, pushing in as realization dawns. A slow push-in on a face intensifies; a pull-out abandons/isolates.

### Blocking / staging
- **Blocking** = the choreography of actors and camera in the space. Good blocking stages the *scene's power dynamics* geographically (who advances, who retreats, who has height). **Depth staging** (actors on multiple planes) beats flat two-shots. Block the actors first, then find the camera — never the reverse.

### The 180-degree rule
- Draw an imaginary **axis (the "line")** between the two key subjects; keep all cameras on **one side**. This preserves consistent screen direction — a character on frame-left stays looking frame-right across cuts, so the audience stays spatially oriented. **Crossing the line** flips screen direction and disorients (occasionally used deliberately to signal confusion or a power reversal). Related: **eyeline consistency**, **screen direction**, **the 30-degree rule** (change angle ≥30° between shots of the same subject or the cut looks like a jump).

### Lighting
- **Three-point lighting** — **Key** (dominant source, ~45° off camera and above eyeline), **Fill** (opposite the key, lifts shadow, controls the **contrast/lighting ratio**), **Back/rim/kicker** (separates subject from background). A starting grammar, not a mandate.
- **High-key** = low ratio, few shadows → bright, comedic/commercial. **Low-key** = high ratio, deep shadows → tense, noir/thriller.
- **Motivated lighting** = light with a *logical in-world reason*. **Practicals** = actual working lights visible in the shot (lamps, neon, candles).
- **Color temperature** (Kelvin): tungsten ~2700–3200K (warm), daylight ~5600K (cool). **Mixing warm foreground + cool background** builds depth and separation ("orange & teal"). Set **white balance** intentionally, not auto.
- The five levers: **contrast ratio, motivated source, quality (hard vs. soft), direction, color.**

### Composition, aspect ratio, exposure
- **Rule of thirds**, **headroom** (too much = subject sinks; too little = claustrophobic; eyes near the upper-third line), **lead room / nose room** (space in the direction of gaze or motion), **leading lines**, **balance/symmetry**, **depth/foreground layering**, **negative space**.
- **Aspect ratio** as authorship: 1.85:1 (standard theatrical), 2.39:1 ('Scope — epic, environmental, isolating within width), 1.66 / 4:3 (intimacy, portraiture, period). The frame shape is a choice.
- **Exposure** intentional: protect highlights, expose for the story point, use tools (waveform, false color, zebras) not the LCD eyeball.

### Common amateur mistakes
- **Flat front lighting** / on-camera light — no shadow, no dimension, no mood.
- **Crossing the 180° line** by accident — disoriented geography.
- **Auto everything** — auto-focus hunting, auto-exposure pumping, auto-white-balance drifting mid-take.
- **Wrong lens for intent** — a wide lens close on a face (distortion), or a zoom when a dolly was needed.
- **Bad headroom / no lead room** — subject crammed in a corner or staring off a frame edge.
- **Unmotivated camera moves** — drifting, roving, "epic drone" energy with no story reason.
- **Overexposed windows / blown highlights**, mixed color temps left uncorrected, no subject/background separation.
- **Shooting coverage flat** — no depth staging, everything on one plane.

## 3. Editing

### Core principle
The cut is where the film is *finally written*. Editing controls **what the audience knows, feels, and looks at, and when.** Meaning is generated **between** shots (Kuleshov), not only within them.

### Continuity & invisible-cut grammar
- **Continuity editing** (classical Hollywood) = making cuts *invisible* so story flows seamlessly: matching action, eyelines, screen direction, and screen space across shots.
- **Cut on motion / cut on action** — cut during a movement (a door opening, a head turn) so the motion carries the eye across the splice and hides it. The single most reliable way to make a cut disappear.
- **Match cuts, match on action, eyeline match** — a shot of a character looking, then a shot of what they see, reads as connected *because of the cut*. **Eyeline match** must respect screen direction (the 180° line lives in the edit too).
- **Coverage** = shooting a scene from enough sizes/angles (master + singles + inserts + reverses) that the editor has the pieces to build rhythm and redirect attention. Under-coverage traps the editor.

### Montage theory
- **Kuleshov effect** — the same neutral face intercut with soup, a coffin, or a woman reads as hunger, grief, or desire. The audience **derives meaning from the juxtaposition**, not the individual shot. The foundational fact of editing: the viewer authors the connection.
- **Eisenstein — montage as collision**: meaning *arises from the collision of independent shots.* Contrasting shots smashed together produce a new **intellectual idea** — the goal is to make the audience *think*. (Five methods: metric, rhythmic, tonal, overtonal, intellectual.) Soviet montage **deliberately draws attention to the cut** — the opposite of invisible continuity.
- Takeaway: continuity hides the cut; montage exploits it. A working editor uses both, knowing which effect they want.

### Pacing, rhythm, when to cut vs. hold
- **Pacing** is emotional, not just fast/slow: cut *rate* should track the scene's internal tension. Cut faster to accelerate/agitate, **hold** to build dread, intimacy, or let a performance land.
- **The hold is a choice** — resisting the cut (letting a shot run) creates discomfort, weight, or realism. Amateurs cut on nerves; pros cut on **intent**.

### Walter Murch — the Rule of Six
An ideal cut satisfies six criteria, **ranked by weight**:
1. **Emotion — 51%** (true to the feeling of the moment?)
2. **Story — 23%** (advance the narrative?)
3. **Rhythm — 10%** (rhythmically right moment?)
4. **Eye-trace — 7%** (respects where the viewer's eye is?)
5. **2D plane / screen direction — 5%** (respects the 180° / planarity)
6. **3D continuity — 4%** (spatial reality of the actual space)

Murch's rule: when a cut can't honor all six, **sacrifice from the bottom up**. Get emotion and story right and the audience forgives errors below. *What audiences finally remember is not the editing — it's how they felt.*

### Assembly → fine cut (the editorial ladder)
Selects/dailies → **assembly** (everything in script order, long) → **rough cut** (shaped, scenes working) → **fine cut** (rhythm, trims, performance nuance) → **picture lock** (hand off to sound, music, VFX, color). Each pass removes; "kill your darlings" is an editorial discipline.

### Common amateur mistakes
- **Cutting on dead air** instead of on motion → visible, jarring splices.
- **Jump cuts** from insufficient angle change (violating the 30° rule) when invisibility was intended.
- **Crossing the line in the edit** — reverse eyelines that flip screen direction.
- **Cutting to the rhythm of the footage rather than the emotion** — technically clean, emotionally dead.
- **Never holding** — reflexive cutting that denies performances room to breathe.
- **Over-covering yet under-selecting** — using every angle because it exists, not because the scene needs it.
- **Fixing story problems with speed** — faster cutting to hide that the scene has no dramatic turn.

## 4. Sound & Music

### Core principle
Sound is **half the picture and most of the emotion**, yet the part audiences least consciously notice. It carries feeling under the radar and it is where cheap productions most obviously betray themselves.

### Diegetic vs. non-diegetic
- **Diegetic** = sound from the story world — dialogue, footsteps, a car horn, a radio a character hears. Source need not be on-screen, only *in the world*.
- **Non-diegetic** = sound the characters **cannot** hear: score, voiceover, a comedic record-scratch. Also called *commentary* sound.
- **Trans-diegetic** = sound that crosses the boundary — a song starts on a character's car radio (diegetic) and swells into the score (non-diegetic). A deliberate, powerful device.
- **Source music** = diegetic music (jukebox, band, radio) vs. **score** = composed non-diegetic music.

### Sound design layers (the mix)
Built in stems: **Dialogue** (production sound + ADR) · **Foley** (human-performed synced sounds — footsteps, cloth, props) · **Sound effects / hard FX** (designed or library — doors, guns, impacts) · **Ambience / atmos / room tone** (the world's background bed) · **Music (score + source)**. Each layer is cut, EQ'd, and balanced so nothing masks the story point.

### Dialogue clarity
The prime directive of the mix: **the audience must understand the words** — *unless a filmmaker deliberately buries them for immersion or POV, a real (if divisive) auteur choice* (Nolan's dense mixes; much sound-forward art cinema). The default is intelligibility; departing from it must be a motivated choice you can name, not an accident. Everything else ducks under dialogue. Clean production sound + **room tone** to smooth edits + ADR only when necessary. Muddy, un-intelligible dialogue *that nobody chose* is the #1 amateur giveaway.

### Silence & dynamics
- **Silence is a tool** — dropping sound out entirely (or to a single tone) makes the audience lean in, signals shock or subjectivity. Its power is relative: silence only lands if the surrounding mix has dynamic range. A wall-to-wall loud mix has no silence to spend.

### How sound carries emotion
Non-diegetic music heightens tension/uplift; diegetic sound **grounds** the viewer in the reality of the scene. The interplay — grounding vs. commenting — is the craft.

### The temp-track trap
During editing, editors lay **temporary music** (usually pulled from other released scores) under scenes. Two dangers: (1) everyone falls in love with the temp and the composer is asked to **plagiarize it**, producing derivative scores; (2) the film's emotion becomes propped up by borrowed music that masks whether the *scene itself* works. Discipline: use temp sparingly, evaluate scenes dry.

### Common amateur mistakes
- **Unintelligible / inconsistent dialogue levels** — the loudest tell of an amateur mix.
- **No room tone / atmos** — dead, vacuum-like silence between lines; edits audibly "pop."
- **Wall-to-wall music** — scoring every second, so nothing means anything and there's no silence left to deploy.
- **On-the-nose scoring** — music duplicating the emotion already on screen ("Mickey-Mousing") instead of adding a counter-layer.
- **Ignoring sound until post** — no location sound plan, no boom discipline, forcing expensive ADR.
- **Temp love** — a score that's just a knockoff of the temp track.
- **Flat, layer-less design** — dialogue and one music track, no foley/ambience, so the world feels unreal.

## 5. Production Design & Costume

### Core principle
Design is **silent storytelling**. Sets, props, color, and wardrobe reveal character, status, era, and theme **before anyone speaks** — and their power depends on **consistency and intention**, not decoration.

### World consistency
Every object implies a world with rules. A believable world is internally coherent — the wear on a costume, the clutter of a room, the era of a prop all agree. Inconsistency (a "period" room with a modern outlet in frame) breaks immersion instantly.

### Color palette
- A **film color palette** is the deliberate, film-wide selection of colors tied to narrative, character, and theme. It is a **collaborative discipline** across DP, production designer, and costume — lighting, sets, and wardrobe must agree or the image reads as accidental.
- **Character coding**: bold/bright = confident, extroverted; muted/desaturated = reserved, repressed. **Color as arc**: a protagonist who begins in bright tones and fades to greys visually dramatizes a spirit breaking — no dialogue required.
- **Color as alliance map**: costume color groups characters into factions the audience reads subconsciously (matching = allied, clashing = opposed).

### Costume signals character & theme
Wardrobe reveals **status, self-image, psychology, and change**: what a character wants to project vs. who they are, tracked through a **costume progression** aligned to the arc (fastened-up and armored → loosened and open). Silhouette, fabric, condition, and fit all speak.

### Vocabulary
Production designer, art director, set decorator, prop master; **set dressing** vs. **props** (a prop is handled by an actor); **color script** (the planned color journey scene-by-scene); **texture, patina, distressing/aging**; **motif** (a recurring visual object/color carrying theme); costume **breakdown** and **continuity**; **silhouette**, **color story**.

### Common amateur mistakes
- **Everything looks "new"** — no distressing/patina, costumes off the rack, sets with no lived-in life.
- **Arbitrary color** — pretty but meaningless; no palette discipline, so factions and arcs read as noise.
- **Design fighting the DP** — wardrobe and set colors chosen without lighting/camera in mind (actors vanishing into the background, skin tones poisoned by wall color).
- **Costume that doesn't change** — no progression tracking the arc.
- **Anachronisms / continuity errors** — modern items in period frames; wardrobe that doesn't match across coverage.
- **Over-decoration** — dressing for "cool" rather than for character truth.

## 6. Performance & Directing Actors

### Core principle
You don't direct **emotions**; you direct **actions**. Emotion is a *result* of a character pursuing a want; give the actor something **playable to do**, and the feeling arrives as a by-product. (Judith Weston, *Directing Actors*.)

### Objective & action verbs
- **Objective** = what the character wants in the scene ("to win her back," "to get him to leave"). **Super-objective** = the want across the whole film.
- **Action verb** = a **transitive verb you do to another person** — *to seduce, to warn, to belittle, to shame, to plead, to disarm.* It has an emotional and a physical component and is **playable**: the actor can *do* it take after take. Contrast with adjectives ("be angry," "be sad") which are **states**, not actions.
- Weston's on-set discipline: before a take, ask each actor **"Do you know what your character wants?"** Playable tools: **verbs, facts, images, events, physical tasks.**

### Playable vs. abstract notes (result direction)
- **Result-oriented direction** = telling the actor the outcome you want to *see/hear* ("be more menacing," "cry here," "more energy") without a why or how. It tells the actor to *indicate* an emotion — producing false, "acted" performances.
- **Playable note** = redirect via action/objective/circumstance: not "be angrier" but *"he just insulted your daughter — make him take it back."* The performance changes because the *pursuit* changed.

### Rehearsal vs. discovery
- **Rehearsal** builds understanding, blocking, and trust — but over-rehearsing can **flatten spontaneity**. Many directors rehearse for comprehension and relationships, then **preserve discovery** for the take (keeping the first live impulse). Balance is per-actor and per-scene.

### Casting
- **Casting is ~90% of directing actors.** The right actor removes most "problems" before day one. Cast for essence and chemistry, not just audition polish; a mis-cast can't be noted into rightness.

### Vocabulary
Objective / super-objective, **action / action verb / intention**, **beat** (a unit where the objective or tactic shifts), **obstacle**, **stakes**, **subtext**, **moment before** (what just happened as the scene opens), **as-if / substitution**, **backstory**, **playable vs. result**, **indicating** (faking emotion externally — pejorative), **hitting the mark**, **eyeline**, ADR/looping.

### Common amateur mistakes
- **Result direction** — "be sad," "more intense," "funnier" — un-actionable adjectives that force indicating.
- **Directing the emotion, not the action** — ignoring that emotion follows need.
- **Over-rehearsing** the life out of a scene, or none at all.
- **Neglecting casting** and trying to fix it with notes.
- **Talking too much / giving notes to both actors publicly** — burying the actor in intellect instead of one clear playable adjustment.
- **No "moment before"** — actors starting cold with no inner life entering the scene.
- **Praising/noting results** ("that was great, do it again") without knowing *why* it worked, so it can't be repeated.

## 7. Producing & Pre-Production

### Core principle
**Prep determines the shoot.** Nearly every problem on set is a decision that should have been made in prep. The stripboard, breakdown, and budget are the instruments that turn a script into an executable, affordable plan — and **the thinking, not the software, is the work.**

### Script breakdown → the plan
- **Script breakdown** = analyzing every scene and **listing every element required** to shoot it — cast, extras, props, wardrobe, vehicles, SFX, stunts, animals, locations — usually color-coded. The atomic unit from which schedule and budget are built. Nothing downstream is trustworthy without it.

### Scheduling & the stripboard
- **Stripboard / production board** = one **color-coded strip per scene**; the AD reorders strips into **shoot days**. A visual model of the entire production.
- **Scheduling logic** (not script order): **minimize company moves**, **group by location**, **maximize daylight**, **work around cast availability**, **respect department prep**, **keep overtime down**, and account for **day/night, INT/EXT**.
- **Every strip has a cost.** Each shoot day carries crew wages, rentals, locations, catering, insurance — **adding one day can add tens of thousands.** A tighter schedule = fewer days = lower budget. The schedule *is* a financial document.

### Budgeting basics
- **Above-the-line** (story rights, producer, director, principal cast) vs. **below-the-line** (crew, equipment, locations, post). Plus **post** and a **contingency** (~10%). The budget is derived *from* the breakdown and schedule, not guessed.
- **The producer's triangle**: scope, time, money — pick your constraints; you can't max all three.

### Vocabulary
Script breakdown / breakdown sheet, **stripboard / strips / board**, **day-out-of-days** (when each actor works), **call sheet** (daily marching orders), **one-liner / one-line schedule**, **shot list & storyboards**, **company move**, **turnaround** (mandated rest between wrap and next call), **above/below the line**, **contingency**, **line producer**, **UPM**, **1st AD** (owns the schedule and set clock), **locations / scout / recce**, **table read**, **department heads (HODs)**.

### Common amateur mistakes
- **Shooting the schedule in script order** — ignoring location grouping and company moves, burning days and money.
- **Skipping the breakdown** — arriving on set missing a prop, an extra, a permit.
- **Over-optimistic day counts** — too many pages/setups per day; the day collapses into overtime or gets abandoned.
- **Budget with no contingency** — no cushion for weather, illness, or overruns.
- **No shot list / storyboard** — improvising coverage on the clock, so the editor gets an incomplete scene.
- **Under-valuing prep to "save money"** — the most expensive false economy in film; prep is the cheapest place to solve a problem.
- **No call sheets / poor communication** — cast and crew arriving without unified information.

---

## The one test across all seven crafts
*Is this choice motivated by story and point of view, or by convenience / decoration / habit?* Intentionality is the line between amateur and professional in every discipline; the vocabulary above exists so a crew can align on that intent quickly.
