# Costume and production design

Two departments that build the visible world. Costume dresses the body; production design dresses everything else — sets, locations, props, paint, dressing. Both answer the same questions the script raises about character, period, class, and theme, and both have to agree with the DP's light and the director's blocking or the frame falls apart.

## Part A — costume design

### Reading a script for character

A costume designer does two passes. First pass is a straight read for story: who these people are, what changes, what the scenes demand (a wedding, a funeral, a fistfight, a month in the same clothes). Second pass is a breakdown — every character, every scene, every change of clothes logged against script day and story day. That breakdown becomes the plot the whole department runs on.

For each character the designer is reading five signals off the page:

- **Silhouette.** The outline before any detail registers. Shoulders, waist, hem, volume. Silhouette carries period faster than fabric does — a 1950s cinched waist and full skirt reads before you see the print. It also carries psychology: a character who takes up space vs. one who folds in. Edith Head built careers on silhouette control; the sheath she put on Grace Kelly in *Rear Window* (1954) says composed, moneyed, in command before Kelly speaks.
- **Colour.** What the character wears against what the world wears. Colour is assigned per character and tracked as an arc (see below). It also has to sit inside the production's palette, so costume and production design agree their colour lists early.
- **Texture.** Wool vs. silk vs. synthetic vs. leather reads as class, labour, and care. Texture is what sells "this person works with their hands" or "this coat cost more than the room." It also photographs — matte absorbs light, sheen throws it, and the DP will care.
- **Period accuracy.** Cut, fabric, fastenings, undergarments, and how worn the piece is. A zip in a pre-1930s garment, a polyester weave in a wool era, a too-clean farmer — each breaks the world for anyone who notices, and someone always notices.
- **Class and status.** Fit, condition, layering, and accessories. New money over-signals; old money under-signals. A tailored garment vs. an inherited one vs. a bought-cheap one tells you the character's whole economic history without a line of dialogue.

### The costume arc

Costume changes across a story the way a character does. The designer plots it as a line, not a set of isolated looks. Common shapes:

- **Colour drift.** A palette that warms or cools as the character changes. In *The Godfather* (1977, designer Anna Hill Johnstone) Michael Corleone moves from military drab and light tones toward the black his father wore as he becomes the Don.
- **Armour on / armour off.** Buttoned and layered when guarded, stripped down when exposed.
- **Assimilation or loss.** A character adopts a new world's clothes, or loses their own. *The Talented Mr. Ripley* runs on Ripley dressing up into a life.
- **Repetition as character.** One outfit worn hard shows time, poverty, or obsession — and doubles as a continuity discipline.

Plot the arc against script days so the audience reads change without being told it.

### Continuity

Continuity is the department's daily grind and the thing that gets noticed when it fails. Every look is photographed (continuity stills), logged, and bagged per character per scene. Track the state of a garment, not just the garment: a shirt that's clean in the master must be equally bloodied in the coverage shot 40 minutes of screen time — three weeks of shooting — later. Multiples of hero garments are made for exactly this: stunt doubles, distressing stages, damage that can't be undone between takes. The script supervisor and costume continuity cross-check. The classic failures are self-inflicted: a tie knot that changes across a cut, a coat buttoned then not, a wound that migrates.

### Colour and the production palette

Costume colour is never chosen in isolation. Production design sets a palette for the world — walls, furniture, cars, sky — and costume places characters inside or against it. Two moves:

- **Blend:** the character belongs to the world, dressed in its colours.
- **Pop:** the character is picked out against it. The girl in the red coat in *Schindler's List* (1993) works because the world around her is monochrome.

The designer, production designer, and DP hold a colour meeting so nobody paints a hero costume the same green as the wall it plays against, or dresses a lead in a colour the LUT will crush.

### Period research

Real research means primary sources: photographs, garments in museum and archive collections, tailoring and pattern books of the era, paintings and catalogues, and living memory when the period is recent enough. The designer learns the underlayers — a period silhouette is built from the corset, crinoline, or foundation garment out, and actors move differently once they're in it. Then the designer decides how strictly to obey. A film can be archivally exact, or period-flavoured and stylised, or deliberately anachronistic (Sofia Coppola's *Marie Antoinette*, 2006, designer Milena Canonero, put Converse in the frame on purpose). The choice is the director's; the designer has to be able to do either and know which they're doing.

### Designers to know

- **Sandy Powell** — range from period exactitude to theatrical excess. *The Young Victoria* (2009), *The Aviator* (2004), *Shakespeare in Love* (1998), *Carol* (2015). Reads class and money precisely; not afraid of spectacle.
- **Ruth Carter** — first Black designer to win the costume Oscar, twice. *Malcolm X* (1992), *Amistad* (1997), *Black Panther* (2018) and its sequel. Builds Afrofuturist and African-rooted design from real cultural research — Wakanda's looks pull from Maasai, Zulu, Himba, and other specific sources.
- **Colleen Atwood** — Burton and Marshall's designer. *Chicago* (2002), *Memoirs of a Geisha* (2005), *Alice in Wonderland* (2010), *Sweeney Todd* (2007). Fantasy and heightened period, strong on construction and craft.
- **Bhanu Athaiya** — first Indian to win an Academy Award, for *Gandhi* (1982). Decades of Hindi cinema across period and contemporary; deep command of the sari and regional Indian dress.
- **Eiko Ishioka** — designer as authored image-maker. *Bram Stoker's Dracula* (1992, Oscar), *The Cell* (2000), *Mirror Mirror* (2012), plus stage and opera. Costume as sculpture and graphic statement rather than realism.

Others worth the shelf: Edith Head (the studio-era standard), Milena Canonero (*Barry Lyndon*, *The Grand Budapest Hotel*), Jacqueline Durran (*Anna Karenina*, *Little Women*), Alexandra Byrne (*Elizabeth: The Golden Age*).

## Part B — production design

The production designer (PD) owns the look of everything that isn't the actor or the light: sets, locations, props, set dressing, colour, texture, graphics, vehicles. Under the PD sit the art director (runs the department day to day and the build), set decorator (dressing), props master, and construction, scenic, and graphics crews. The PD reports to the director and works hand in glove with the DP, because a set exists to be lit and shot.

### World-building through sets, locations, and props

The job is to make a coherent place the story can happen in. Every set answers who lives or works here, what they can afford, what they care about, and how long they've been here. A room is a biography: what's on the walls, what's worn, what's new, what's missing. Locations are chosen or dressed to the same standard; a found location usually gets stripped and re-dressed rather than shot as-is. Props divide into hero props (handled, seen close, story-critical — often built in multiples) and dressing props (fill the world, seen in passing).

### The colour script

A colour script maps the film's colour scene by scene or sequence by sequence, so colour moves with the story instead of being set-dressed at random. Pixar formalised the practice for animation, but live-action PDs plan the same way — assigning palettes to acts, locations, or emotional beats and controlling how they shift. The PD's colour script has to agree with costume's colour arc and the DP's lighting and grade; these are one conversation, not three.

### Set dressing

Set decoration is the layer that makes a built or found space look lived-in. The set decorator sources and places furniture, rugs, art, plants, books, dishes, the contents of drawers a camera may never open (actors open them; the honesty shows). Dressing is where character detail lives at the finest grain — the specific magazines, the brand of cigarettes, the state of the dishes. Greens (plants and landscape) and graphics (signage, packaging, newspapers, screens) are specialist dressing crews under the same department.

### Design supporting theme

Design carries theme when the physical world argues the film's idea. Ken Adam's war room in *Dr. Strangelove* (1964) — the black expanse, the ring of light, the circular table — is the film's argument about power and annihilation built as a room. Production design does this through scale, symmetry, decay, order vs. chaos, and how much the world hems the characters in. The rule is the same as costume: let the set say it so the dialogue doesn't have to.

### Designers to know

- **Ken Adam** — the Bond production designer and *Dr. Strangelove*. Vast modernist sets, hard geometry, the villain's lair as an idea about power. Won for *Barry Lyndon* (1975) and *The Madness of King George* (1994).
- **Rick Carter** — Spielberg and Zemeckis's designer. *Jurassic Park* (1993), *Forrest Gump* (1994), *Avatar* (2009, Oscar), *Lincoln* (2012, Oscar). Range from photoreal period to invented worlds.
- **Hannah Beachler** — first Black winner of the production design Oscar, for *Black Panther* (2018). Built Wakanda as a coherent nation with an internal history — her "Wakanda bible" documented the culture, tech, and geography behind the design. Also *Moonlight* (2016), *Creed* (2015).
- **Dante Ferretti** — Scorsese, Fellini, and Pasolini's designer. *The Age of Innocence* (1993), *Gangs of New York* (2002), *The Aviator* (2004, Oscar), *Hugo* (2011, Oscar), *Sweeney Todd* (2007, Oscar). Grand period build at the top of the craft.

Others worth the shelf: Stuart Craig (the *Harry Potter* films), Patrice Vermette (*Dune*), Adam Stockhausen (Wes Anderson), Sarah Greenwood (Joe Wright's films), Nathan Crowley (Nolan's designer).

## Part C — the costume analyzer (runnable method)

Given a character description or a described outfit, run these six passes and return notes plus fixes. Ask for anything you need before judging — period, genre, the production palette, the character's arc position — but if it's not given, state the assumption and analyze anyway.

**Inputs to gather (or assume):** character (who they are, class, arc position), the outfit or set as described, period/setting, genre, the production palette, and the scene's story day.

**Pass 1 — character read.** Does the outfit read who this person is without dialogue? Check silhouette, colour, texture, fit, condition, and accessories against the character's class, job, and psychology. Note anything that contradicts the character (a broke character in pristine tailoring) or says nothing (generic, could be anyone).

**Pass 2 — period and setting.** Check cut, fabric, fastenings (zips, Velcro, snaps have datable histories), undergarments implied by the silhouette, footwear, and grooming against the stated period. Flag anachronisms. When you're unsure of a date — when a fabric, fastening, or garment type entered use — say so and recommend a source check rather than guessing.

**Pass 3 — genre fit.** Does the look sit inside its genre's language? Noir wants shadow-friendly tailoring; a western wants earned wear and weather; heightened fantasy can break realism on purpose. Note where the outfit fights the genre.

**Pass 4 — palette fit.** Place the outfit's colours against the production palette. Is the character meant to blend or pop, and does this achieve that? Flag a hero colour that clashes with a key set colour, or a lead who disappears into the background when they shouldn't. Check it survives the likely grade (very dark or very saturated garments can crush or bloom).

**Pass 5 — continuity.** Against the character's other scenes and their arc: does this look track? Is the garment's state (clean/worn/damaged) right for the story day? Does it advance or break the colour arc? Flag anything that contradicts an earlier or later scene.

**Pass 6 — practical.** Will it photograph and move? Note fabrics that strobe on camera (tight patterns, herringbone), pure white or deep black that fights exposure, sheen that throws hot spots, and anything that restricts the blocking or stunt the scene needs. Note whether multiples are required (damage, stunts, doubles).

**Output format:**

```
CHARACTER / OUTFIT: <one line>
ASSUMPTIONS: <period, genre, palette, arc position — whatever you had to assume>

READS THE CHARACTER: yes / partly / no — <why>
PERIOD: pass / flags — <specifics>
GENRE: pass / flags — <specifics>
PALETTE: blend / pop / clash — <specifics>
CONTINUITY: <what tracks, what breaks>
PRACTICAL: <camera and movement notes>

FIXES:
1. <specific, actionable change>
2. ...
```

Keep fixes concrete: name the garment, the colour, the change. "Swap the polyester shirt for a period-correct cotton in a duller blue so he blends with the office set and reads working-class" beats "make it more period."

## Part D — prop list and set-dressing method

**Prop list.** Break the script scene by scene and pull every object a character touches or the story names. Sort into:

- **Hero props** — handled on camera, seen close, or story-critical (the gun, the letter, the ring, the phone that must work). These get sourced or built, cleared for rights if branded, and made in multiples if they're consumed, damaged, or used in stunts.
- **Dressing props** — fill the world, seen in passing, not handled (books on a shelf, bottles on a bar).
- **Action props** — do something mechanical (a weapon that fires, a device that lights up, food that gets eaten).

For each hero prop log: scene(s), description, quantity and why, source (buy / rent / build), practical needs (must function, must break, must be safe), rights/clearance, and continuity state across scenes. Flag period accuracy the same as costume — a wrong-era object in a hero's hand is as loud as a wrong-era coat.

**Set dressing.** Work a set from the character out. Ask who uses this space, their money, their taste, their history, how long they've been here, and what just happened in it. Then build layers:

1. **Big pieces** — furniture, appliances, rugs, the fixed and large.
2. **Mid layer** — lamps, art, plants (greens), textiles, the things on surfaces.
3. **Fine detail** — the specific, character-level objects: the mail on the counter, the brand on the shelf, the photo on the desk, the wear on the armrest. This layer is where the set stops being a set.
4. **Graphics** — signage, packaging, screens, newspapers, anything with type, cleared or original-designed to avoid rights problems.

Dress to the lens: know the camera's angles and lens before over-dressing walls the frame never sees, but dress drawers and corners anyway because actors find them and the truth carries. Photograph the dressed set for continuity before shooting, and cross-check the set's palette against the costume colours playing in it so a lead doesn't vanish into the sofa.
