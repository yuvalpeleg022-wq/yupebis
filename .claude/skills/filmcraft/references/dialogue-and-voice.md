# Dialogue and voice

Dialogue is what characters say to get what they want from each other. It is behavior, not information delivery. When it goes wrong, everyone sounds like the writer, states exactly what they feel, and explains the plot to people who already know it. This doc covers how to make characters sound like different people and how to strip the lines that give the writing away. Examples are before/after so the fix is concrete.

## Subtext: people rarely say the thing

Subtext is the gap between what a line says and what the character means. Real speech is indirect because people protect themselves, angle for advantage, and assume the other person already knows the context. Harold Pinter built a career on the space under the words; the famous Pinter pause is subtext made audible.

The test: if a character says exactly what they feel and want, plainly, you have on-the-nose dialogue and no subtext. Fix it by giving the character a reason to hide, deflect, or attack sideways.

**Before (on the nose):**
> ANNA: I'm angry that you forgot our anniversary, and I feel unloved and taken for granted.

**After (subtext):**
> ANNA: There's cake in the fridge. I bought it yesterday. For no reason.

The anger, the hurt, the "you forgot" are all there, carried by "for no reason," which means the opposite. The audience does the work, which is why it lands.

## Tactics: dialogue as action (Mamet)

David Mamet's core idea, laid out in *On Directing Film* and drilled in *Glengarry Glen Ross*, is that a character is not a bundle of feelings but a set of actions aimed at an objective. Each line is a **tactic**, a move to get something from the other person: to flatter, threaten, shame, seduce, stall, bait. When a scene dies, it's usually because nobody wants anything, or everybody wants it politely.

Write the scene by asking, for each character: what do they want in this scene, and what are they willing to do to get it? Then let the tactics collide. The words are just the current tool; when a tactic fails, the character switches to another, and that switch is where drama lives.

**Before (feelings, no tactic):**
> MARCUS: I really need this job. Please consider me. I'd be grateful.

**After (tactics, shifting):**
> MARCUS: You've got three guys out there who'll quit in a month. (beat) I've got a kid. I don't quit anything.

Marcus wants the job. Tactic one: undercut the competition. Tactic two: raise the personal stake as leverage. He never says "please" and he's stronger for it.

Mamet's discipline also kills stage directions that do the acting: don't write "(desperately)"; write a line that can only be said desperately.

## Per-character voice: making people sound different

If you can swap two characters' lines without noticing, they don't have voices. Differentiate along measurable axes:

- **Vocabulary and register.** Blue-collar vs. academic, plain Anglo-Saxon words vs. Latinate ones, profanity or its studied absence. A character who says "commence" is not the same person as one who says "get going."
- **Sentence rhythm and length.** Some people talk in long spooling clauses; some in clipped fragments. Aaron Sorkin's characters overlap and sprint; Cormac McCarthy's speak in short, unpunctuated declaratives. The rhythm is the fingerprint.
- **Idiolect.** The private tics: a repeated filler ("look,", "the thing is"), a signature curse, a refusal to swear, a pet metaphor drawn from their job (a mechanic reaching for engine metaphors, a nurse for the body).
- **What they won't say.** Silence is characterization. A person who never says "I love you," never asks for help, never admits fear tells you who they are by the hole.
- **Directness vs. indirection.** Some characters state; some circle. Pair a stater with a circler and every scene between them has friction for free.

**Before (two undifferentiated voices):**
> COP A: We should check the warehouse. It seems suspicious.
> COP B: Yes, I agree. The warehouse is suspicious. Let's go.

**After (differentiated):**
> RIOS: Warehouse. Been dark three weeks, now there's a truck out front twice a day.
> DELANEY: Or somebody's storing patio furniture. (already putting his coat on) But sure. Let's ruin our afternoon.

Rios states facts, no filler, cop-brain. Delaney deflects with a joke while doing the thing anyway. You could not swap those lines.

A practical trick: write a one-page monologue for each major character on a subject unrelated to the plot (their worst job, their opinion on a food). If they all sound the same, the voices aren't built yet.

## Stripping on-the-nose lines

On-the-nose dialogue says the meaning out loud. It shows up as:

- **Emotional narration:** "I'm scared." Cut it; show the tactic fear produces.
- **Naming the theme:** "This is really about trust, isn't it?" Never let a character diagnose the movie.
- **Explaining a relationship the audience can see:** "You're my brother and I've always resented you."
- **Answering the question you were asked:** real people dodge. A dodged question characterizes and creates tension.

**Before:**
> DAD: I'm proud of you, son. I know I don't say it enough, and I've been distant since your mother died, but I love you.

**After:**
> DAD: (looking at the diploma, not the son) Your mother would've made a whole thing of this. (beat) You want a beer or you too fancy now.

The pride, the grief, the distance, and the reaching-out are all in there, and none of them are stated. "You want a beer" is how this man says "I love you."

## Exposition as a tactic inside conflict

Exposition is information the audience needs. It goes flat when characters tell each other things they already know ("As you know, Bob, our company has been bankrupt since the fire"). The fix, credited to a lot of people but cleanest in the "As you know" rule: never deliver information as information. Deliver it as a weapon, a plea, a lie, a boast, inside a scene where someone wants something.

**Before (inert exposition):**
> LENA: As you know, our father left the company to me in the will, and you've been contesting it for two years because you think you deserve half.

**After (exposition as attack):**
> GABE: Two years, Lena. Two years of lawyers.
> LENA: Then stop paying them. It's my name on the will. It was always going to be my name.
> GABE: He was drugged to the eyeballs when he signed it and you know it.

Same facts, the will, the two-year fight, the contested competence, but they arrive as ammunition in a fight the characters are actively losing and winning. Nobody explains; everybody attacks.

Two more tools:

- **Withhold, then reveal under pressure.** Let the audience feel the gap before you fill it. Information dragged out of a reluctant character is worth ten times the same information volunteered.
- **Spread it thin.** One fact per beat, delivered while the scene is doing something else.

## Dialect and accent on the page

Write dialect at the level of word choice and rhythm, not phonetic spelling. Heavy eye-dialect ("Ah reckon Ah cain't") is hard to read, condescending, and dates badly. Zora Neale Hurston used it deliberately and well; most writers who try it produce mud.

The reliable approach:

- **Syntax and idiom over spelling.** An Irish rhythm comes from clause order and "amn't I," not from spelling every vowel differently. A specific region's slang, filler, and sentence shape carry the accent; let the actor supply the sound.
- **A light touch of signature word.** One or two markers per character (a Scots "aye," a regional term of address) cue the accent; a page of it exhausts.
- **Trust the casting and the actor.** You write the music; they play it. Over-notating accent is a form of not trusting the room.

**Before (eye-dialect mud):**
> "Ah dunno whatchu talkin' 'bout, we ain't nevva been down dere."

**After (syntax and idiom):**
> "I don't know what you mean. We've never once been down there, either of us."

Cast the accent; let the actor deliver it. The rhythm ("either of us" tagged on the end) does more than the spelling did.

## Silence, interruption, overlap

Speech is not tidy turn-taking. Use the mechanics:

- **Interruption** shows power, urgency, or contempt. Mark it with an em dash cut-off. Who interrupts whom is a status map.
- **Overlap** (Altman's signature, *Nashville*, *McCabe & Mrs. Miller*) makes a scene feel alive and lets you bury exposition where the audience half-catches it, which is often enough.
- **Silence and the non-answer.** A character who says nothing, or answers a different question, is loud. The Pinter pause is a held tactic, not empty air.

**Before (polite turn-taking):**
> A: I think we should sell the house.
> B: I understand your point, but I disagree.

**After (interruption, status):**
> A: I think we should sell the ---
> B: No.
> A: You didn't ---
> B: The answer's no.

B holds all the power and we never had to say so.

## The table read as diagnostic

A table read (cast around a table, reading the script aloud, no acting-out) is the cheapest, fastest dialogue test there is. It exposes what the page hides. Listen for:

- **Interchangeable voices.** If you close your eyes and can't tell who's speaking, the voices aren't differentiated. Flag every line that could belong to anyone.
- **Lines that die in the mouth.** Dialogue that scans on the page but no actor can say naturally. Usually too written, too balanced, too complete.
- **On-the-nose clunkers** that made sense silently and land like a brick out loud.
- **Dead scenes** where nobody's pursuing anything, audible as a flat, informational patch.
- **Rhythm problems.** Everyone talking at the same tempo, or a scene with no variation in line length.
- **Laughs and silences in the wrong places,** which tell you where the intended effect isn't landing.

You can simulate a table read alone or with the `dialogue-doctor` agent: read every character's lines aloud in a distinct voice and flag every line you could reassign to another character without loss. Those flagged lines are your voice-differentiation work list. Then flag every line that states a feeling, names the theme, or explains something two characters both know, that's your on-the-nose pass. Rewrite both lists as tactics with subtext, then read again.

## Quick checklist

- Does each character want something specific in the scene, and pursue it with tactics?
- Can you swap two characters' lines unnoticed? If yes, build the voices.
- Is anyone saying their feeling out loud? Cut it; show the behavior.
- Is exposition arriving as a weapon in a conflict, or as a lecture?
- Is dialect in the syntax, or drowned in phonetic spelling?
- Read it aloud. The lines that die in your mouth die on screen.
