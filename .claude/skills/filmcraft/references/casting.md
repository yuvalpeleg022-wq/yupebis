# Casting

Casting decides more of a film's outcome than any single craft choice after the script. The wrong lead sinks a good script; the right unknown can carry a thin one. This is how casting actually works, from breakdown to offer, across markets. Money and availability figures below drift constantly. Today is 2026-07-20; treat every fee, deal, and "available" claim here as a starting reference to verify before relying.

## What a casting director actually does

A casting director (CD) is not a talent agent and does not represent actors. The CD works for production, is hired by the director and producers, and is paid a flat fee plus (sometimes) a small back-end. Marion Dougherty, who effectively invented the modern American CD role, cast against studio type on purpose (Dustin Hoffman and Jon Voight in *Midnight Cowboy*, Al Pacino before anyone knew the name). Her successors, people like Juliet Taylor (Woody Allen's films for decades), Ellen Lewis (Scorsese), Avy Kaufman, Francine Maisler, Nina Gold (*Game of Thrones*, *The Crown*), and Kharmel Cochrane in the UK, do the same job: turn a script into a room of real, available human beings.

The day-to-day:

- Break the script into roles and write breakdowns.
- Build lists (the "wish list" and the realistic list) per role.
- Reach out to agents to check interest and availability.
- Run auditions, self-tapes, callbacks, and chemistry reads.
- Negotiate the early shape of deals (agents and business affairs close them).
- Manage the offer/hold/pass sequence so the schedule doesn't collapse.
- Read the room politically: who the director wants, who the financiers require, who the streamer's algorithm rewards.

The CD's real value is taste plus a Rolodex plus knowing who is actually free in the window you need them. That last part is unglamorous and decisive.

## Breakdowns and sides

A **breakdown** is the role's spec sheet sent to agents and managers. In the US this runs through Breakdown Services (founded by Gary Marsh, 1971), the industry clearinghouse agents subscribe to. A breakdown names the project, the role, age range, a one-line character description, rate/contract type (SAG-AFTRA scale, guest star, series regular, etc.), shoot dates, and location. Good breakdowns describe behavior, not adjectives: "runs a pawn shop, lies for a living, has never been caught" beats "morally ambiguous, complex."

**Sides** are the pages an actor reads from, one to three scenes chosen to expose the role's spine. Casting picks sides that force a choice: a scene where the character wants something and hits resistance. Bad sides are exposition dumps where any competent actor sounds the same. Good sides separate people fast.

**Self-tapes** are now the default first round (accelerated by the 2020 shutdown and never reversed). An actor records the sides at home to a reader and uploads. This widened the funnel (geography stopped mattering for round one) and pushed more unpaid labor onto actors, a live SAG-AFTRA grievance. Callbacks and chemistry reads still happen in person or on Zoom.

## Offers, quotes, availability, holds

This system exists to solve one problem: you cannot build a schedule around a "maybe."

- **Availability check ("avail" / "check avail"):** casting asks the agent whether the actor is free in the shoot window and interested in principle. No commitment. Agents use avails to gauge heat and start leverage.
- **Offer:** a formal, usually written, commitment to hire at stated terms if accepted. In the US an "offer only" role means the actor won't audition; you offer or you don't. Stars work offer-only.
- **Quote:** the actor's most recent fee on a comparable project, tracked by agents as a floor for the next negotiation. "Her quote is X" means she last got X; going below needs a reason (passion project, prestige director, festival play). Quotes ratchet up and rarely down, which is why a flop-after-flop star can still command a stale high quote until the market corrects.
- **Hold / pin / release:** a **hold** (or "pin," "first refusal") reserves an actor's dates informally. **First position** means your project has priority on those dates; **second position** means another project outranks you and you shoot around them. If a competing offer lands, the agent calls for a **release**: you either upgrade to a firm offer within a deadline or let the actor go.
- **Pay-or-play:** once a deal is pay-or-play, you owe the fee whether or not the film gets made or the role gets cut. Stars insist on it; it's the main reason a canceled production still bleeds money.

The sequence in practice: check avail on your top choice, offer if they're warm, hold your second choice in case the first passes, release the hold the moment the first closes. Botch the timing and both walk.

## Chemistry reads and pairing logic

A chemistry read puts two (or more) actors in a room to see if the relationship reads on camera. It's mandatory for romantic leads and buddy pairings and increasingly for found-family ensembles. You are not testing whether they like each other; you're testing whether the audience will believe the specific relationship, love, rivalry, dependence, contempt.

What CDs watch for:

- **Listening.** Do they change based on what the other person does, or run their prepared reading over the top of it?
- **Physical ease or friction** appropriate to the relationship. Forced closeness reads as forced.
- **Status play.** Who's up, who's down, and does it move? A flat status line kills a scene between equals-who-shouldn't-be.
- **Contrast that complements.** Redford/Newman worked on opposite rhythms. Pairing two identical energies flattens; pairing two clashing ones can spark or can just clash.

Pairing logic beyond romance: comedic duos need a straight man and a wild card (the "funny/less-funny" axis); rivals need believable parity so the outcome stays in doubt; mentor/protégé needs an age and status gap the audience feels without dialogue explaining it.

## Star vs. character actor vs. discovery

Every role sits somewhere on this triangle, and the choice is strategic, not just aesthetic.

- **Star (bankable name):** finances the film, opens it, reassures financiers and distributors, brings a pre-sold audience and a persona. Costs the most, may overpower a role the audience should meet fresh, and drags their off-screen baggage into every scene. Use when the film needs to be sold before it's seen (international pre-sales, wide theatrical).
- **Character actor:** the specialist who disappears into parts and makes small roles land. Cheaper, deeper, lower risk on performance, near-zero marketing value. Think the working ensemble players who anchor prestige TV. Use to fill everything around the leads and to buy credibility.
- **Discovery (unknown / newcomer):** the audience meets the role with no baggage, total surprise. Highest performance risk, longest search, near-zero pre-sale value, potentially the film's whole story if it lands (Gabourey Sidibe in *Precious*, cast off an open call). Use when the role must feel like a real, unfamiliar person and the film can be sold on other elements.

Most films blend all three: a bankable lead or two, a bench of character actors, and one discovery in a role that benefits from freshness.

## Star quotes and bankability

Bankability is the honest question of how much money a name reliably brings in, and it is more fragile than the trades pretend. Historically distributors used ranked lists (the old *Ulmer Scale*; agency internal models now) that fold together box-office track record, international appeal, genre fit, and social reach. In the streaming era, "bankable" increasingly means measurable engagement (completion rates, subscriber acquisition) that platforms hold privately, which has moved leverage from talent to platforms.

Quotes track the last comparable fee and function as a negotiating floor. A "$20M-against-20%-of-first-dollar-gross" back-end structure is largely gone from the 2010s; the streaming buyout model (large upfront cash-out in exchange for no back-end) replaced it for many titles, then partially reversed after talent revolts (the *Black Widow* dispute being the loud example). Net: there is no single "the quote" for a major star anymore. It's project-shaped. Verify any figure live before relying, and remember the number an agent floats is a target, not a fact.

## Ensemble balance

An ensemble is a portfolio, not a pile of good actors. Balance across:

- **Register.** If everyone plays big, nothing is big. Cast a couple of low, still presences to make the loud ones read.
- **Physical and vocal distinctness.** The audience must tell people apart in a wide shot and in one line of dialogue. Casting two similar-looking, similar-sounding mid-40s men in the same scene is a continuity headache and a story cost.
- **Age and status spread** that matches the story's structure, not just realism.
- **Give-and-take heat.** Some actors generate, some absorb. A room of only generators exhausts; a room of only absorbers goes slack.

Nina Gold's *Game of Thrones* work is the reference case: a huge cast kept legible because faces, voices, and registers were deliberately spread.

## Regional casting

Casting norms are not universal. Know the market you're in.

- **Hollywood / US:** SAG-AFTRA governs; Breakdown Services distributes; self-tapes dominate round one; offer-only for stars; agencies (CAA, WME, UTA) and managers gate access. Diversity mandates and inclusion riders (below) now shape studio and streamer lists.
- **Bollywood and regional Indian cinema:** historically star-and-family driven (the Kapoors, Khans, Bachchans; Deol, Devgn lines), with "star kids" debuting through production relationships more than open casting, a live public controversy about nepotism. That said, casting directors have professionalized fast: Mukesh Chhabra (*Gangs of Wasseypur*, and the ecosystem around Anurag Kashyap's films) built breakdown-and-audition pipelines that surfaced outsiders. Regional industries diverge sharply: Tamil, Telugu (Tollywood), Malayalam (often the most performance-first and least star-gated), Kannada, Bengali, each with its own star systems, fee scales, and language-fluency requirements. A Telugu tentpole (Rajamouli-scale) casts for pan-Indian dubbing reach; a Malayalam realist drama casts for texture and often uses non-actors.
- **Korean (K-drama / film):** agency and management companies (often tied to broadcasters and, for idols, to entertainment labels) control access. Idol-to-actor crossover casting is a business decision about fandom-driven ratings and international streaming (Netflix Korea). Chemistry between leads is scrutinized publicly by fandoms and factored in commercially.
- **Chinese (C-drama / film):** heavy star systems, "traffic stars" (流量, high social-media draw) weighed against acting ability, and a regulatory layer, censorship, "morality" clauses, and periodic state guidance can force a name off a finished project, so productions build in replacement risk. Verify current regulatory constraints live; they change without warning.
- **European:** more director-and-CD driven, less agency-gated, national funding bodies and co-production rules (a French-German co-pro may need cast from both territories to unlock funding). Casting directors like Des Hamilton (UK, street-cast realism for Andrea Arnold) and the tradition around the Dardennes' non-professional leads sit at the opposite end from the star system.

## Diversity and authenticity

Two related questions: who gets seen, and who gets to play whom.

- **Inclusion rider:** a contract clause (popularized by Stacy Smith's USC Annenberg research and Frances McDormand's 2018 Oscar speech) requiring the cast and crew to reflect real demographics. It moves the default list, not just the final pick.
- **Authenticity casting:** the argument that specific lived experience (deaf actors for deaf roles, as in *CODA*; trans actors for trans roles; disabled actors for disabled roles) belongs to people with that experience, on both ethical and craft grounds. The counter-argument (acting is impersonation) has lost ground since the mid-2010s, and mis-casting now carries real reputational and commercial risk. Practically: widen the search rather than defaulting to the nearest name, and build relationships with the community-specific talent pipelines that make an authentic search possible.
- **Colorblind vs. conscious casting:** "colorblind" (ignore race in casting) and "color-conscious" (cast race deliberately for meaning) are different tools. Neither is automatically right; the choice depends on whether the character's identity is load-bearing in the story.

The craft point underneath the politics: a wider, more deliberate search produces better, less generic casts. The lazy default (same twelve names per age bracket) is where flat casting comes from.

## A runnable method: build a tiered suggestion list

Given a role, produce four tiers with reasoning per name, then verify. This is the procedure the `casting-scout` agent automates; you can run it by hand.

**Inputs you must fix first:**
1. The role's spine in one sentence (what they want, what's in the way, one true contradiction).
2. Target market/region (Hollywood, a specific Indian-language industry, Korea, Europe, etc.), this changes the entire name pool.
3. Budget tier (micro / indie / mid / studio-tentpole), this caps which tiers are reachable.
4. The shoot window (availability is meaningless without dates).

**Then build the tiers:**

- **A-list / bankable.** 2–4 names who could finance or open the film. For each: why the persona fits the role, what they'd bring to pre-sales, and the risk (cost, overexposure, baggage). Note these are offer-only and pay-or-play.
- **Rising.** 3–5 names on the way up, one hit from A-list, cheaper now, hungry for the right part. For each: the recent role that proves the range, and why this part extends rather than repeats it.
- **Character / working actors.** 3–6 names who will nail it on performance with low risk and low cost. For each: the specific quality (stillness, menace, warmth) the role needs and where they've shown it.
- **Discovery.** A description of the type to search for and 1–2 concrete channels (open call, a specific regional theater scene, a community pipeline for authenticity roles). For each: why freshness serves this particular role.

**For every name, state the reasoning, not just the name.** "She plays competence-hiding-panic better than anyone in that age range; see [recent role]" is usable. A bare list is not.

**Then the mandatory caveat.** Availability, current quotes, and interest change weekly and cannot be known from a static document. Every suggestion is a hypothesis. Before relying on any name, check live: are they free in the window, what's their current quote, are they offer-only, what are they attached to right now. Mark the whole list "verify before relying" and date it (today: 2026-07-20).

**Worked shape (illustrative, not current):** for a lead role of "a burned-out defense lawyer who still can't stop winning," an A-list tier might argue for a mid-career star known for controlled anger; the rising tier for someone who just did a breakout courtroom-adjacent role; the character tier for a reliable player who does exhausted-competence in his sleep; the discovery tier for "search regional stage actors 45–55 with real trial-lawyer bearing." The names matter less than the reasoning and the discipline of checking them live.

## Common failure modes

- Casting the wish list and never checking avail, so the schedule is built on people who were never free.
- Confusing quote (a target) with cost (what you'll actually pay).
- Ignoring second-position holds and losing an actor to a competing shoot.
- A cast with no register contrast, everyone at the same volume.
- Defaulting to the nearest twelve names and calling the result "the industry standard," which is how identical, forgettable casts happen.
- Stating "available" or a precise fee as fact from stale information. It is always a live question.
