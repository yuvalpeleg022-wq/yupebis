# Development and market

How a film goes from a sentence to a greenlight, and how to judge whether the market will pay for it. Development is the cheapest part of a film to fix and the most expensive to skip. Every dollar of a script problem multiplies on the shoot.

Figures in this doc (box office, budgets, quote levels, streamer license fees) drift constantly. Today is 2026-07-20. Pull current numbers live before you rely on any of them — see "Live data" at the bottom.

## The development pipeline

Idea → logline → treatment → script → package → greenlight. Each stage is a gate. You do not pay for the next stage until the current one holds.

1. **Idea.** A premise, a "what if," a character in a situation, a true story, a piece of IP. Cheap and worthless until pressure-tested. Most ideas die here and should.
2. **Logline.** One or two sentences. If you cannot write the logline, you do not have the movie yet — you have a mood. Robert McKee and Blake Snyder both argue that if the logline does not sell, the script will not either.
3. **Treatment / outline.** Two to twelve pages of prose telling the whole story present-tense, beginning to end, with the ending on the page. This is where structure breaks are found and fixed for free. Pixar's "story spine" and the beat-sheet approaches (Snyder's *Save the Cat*, John Truby's *The Anatomy of Story*) live here.
4. **Script.** The first draft is for the writer. The second is for the story. The third is for the reader — the exec, the actor, the financier who reads 200 scripts a year and quits on page 10 if nothing is happening. Screenwriter William Goldman's rule: enter every scene late, leave early.
5. **Package.** Script + director + one or two cast + a finance/sales anchor. A script alone rarely gets made. A package is what a financier actually buys.
6. **Greenlight.** The money commits. From here, development notes get expensive. Everything above should have been resolved before this line.

Development hell is what happens when a project cycles between stages 4 and 5 without ever clearing either gate — usually because the logline was never true.

### The reader's mindset

Nobody in the chain reads your script the way you wrote it. A studio reader, an agent's assistant, a financier's development person reads fast, tired, and looking for a reason to stop. The industry coverage form they fill out grades premise, structure, character, dialogue, and gives a PASS / CONSIDER / RECOMMEND verdict. Most scripts get a PASS on the first read and never get a second. Write for that reader: a first ten pages that establishes tone and a question, a clear engine by the end of the first act, and no scene that a fast reader would skim. Franklin Leonard built the Black List around the observation that good scripts were being lost in this pile, not that they did not exist.

### Treatment and outline craft

The treatment is where you find out if the story works before it costs anything. Write it present tense, third person, telling the whole story including the ending — never "and then the hero must make a choice that will change everything." Say what the choice is and what changes. The disciplines that live here:

- **Structure.** Whether you use three-act, Snyder's 15-beat *Save the Cat* sheet, Truby's 22 steps, or the sequence method (eight ~15-minute units taught by Frank Daniel), the point is the same: know where the turns are and whether the midpoint actually turns. A midpoint that is just "more of the same" is the most common structural failure.
- **The promise of the premise.** Snyder's phrase for the section (roughly the fun-and-games of act two) that delivers what the poster sold. A body-swap comedy has to be funny about swapped bodies here. If the treatment skips it, the movie has no reason to exist.
- **Character want vs. need.** The external goal the character pursues and the internal lack they do not yet see. The best endings pay off both at once. If your treatment only has a plot, the film will feel hollow no matter how well shot.

## Budget tiers and what they demand

A project's budget tier is not a detail — it decides which market you are in, which talent matters, and how the film must earn back. Rough tiers (verify current thresholds, they move):

- **Micro / no-budget (under ~$1M).** Concept and execution carry everything; no star, so the hook must sell. Festival-and-platform path. Comps are other micro-budget breakouts, and the honest average is close to zero — the wins are outliers.
- **Low-budget indie (~$1–10M).** One recognizable name plus a strong hook. Sales agents and festival premieres do the work. This is where the greenlight scorecard's budget-to-market fit axis bites hardest.
- **Mid-budget (~$10–40M).** The squeezed middle — too expensive for a pure festival play, too cheap for a wide tentpole. Needs real cast value or a genre with a reliable audience (horror, faith, elevated thriller). Streamers absorbed much of this tier.
- **Studio / tentpole (~$100M+).** IP, franchise, or event scale. Wide theatrical, P&A that rivals the budget, four-quadrant reach. A different business entirely.

Comp to your own tier. The most common development self-deception is a mid-budget script comped to tentpole outcomes.

## Pitching the project

The written logline sells on the page; a live pitch sells in a room. Structure a verbal pitch as: the hook (one line), the world and protagonist, the engine of act one, the shape of the story without every beat, the ending's emotional payoff, then the comps, budget tier, and why now. Keep it under a few minutes and stop talking. Producer and financier notes almost always come back to the same two questions — "who is this for" and "why now" — so answer both before they ask. Have the answer to "what does this cost and what does it make" ready, and have it pulled from live comps, not from hope.

## Logline lab

A logline is not a summary. It is the engine of the story compressed to one line: who wants what, what stands in the way, and what is at stake.

### The formula

> When [inciting incident], a [flawed protagonist with an adjective] must [active goal] before [stakes / ticking clock], or else [consequence].

Working example: "When a phobic ex-cop learns a bomber has rigged a city bus to explode if it drops below 50 mph, he must keep it moving through gridlocked LA long enough to find and stop the bomber." (*Speed*.) It has an engine, a clock, a clear want, and a visible obstacle.

### What a strong logline carries

- **An active protagonist** with a specific flaw or gap. "A man" is dead. "A washed-up magician who hasn't done a real trick in years" is alive.
- **Irony or a hook** — the built-in contradiction that makes people lean in. A hitman who can't kill. A wedding planner who can't fall in love.
- **A visible goal** the audience can watch the character pursue.
- **Stakes and a clock.** What is lost if they fail, and why now.
- **Genre legible in the wording.** A comedy logline should read funny; a horror logline should read scary.

### Common failures

- **Mood, not engine.** "A meditation on grief in the American Midwest." Beautiful, unsellable — no want, no obstacle.
- **Passive protagonist.** Things happen *to* the hero instead of the hero *doing*. Fix: give them a goal by the end of the sentence.
- **No irony.** If the premise has no built-in contradiction, it reads generic.
- **Two movies.** The logline tries to carry an A-plot and a B-plot. Cut to the spine.
- **Cast-and-crew laundry list.** Naming five characters. Name one, maybe two.
- **The ending hidden.** In a pitch logline you protect the twist; in a *development* logline (for yourself, the director, the financier) put the real story on the page.

### Iteration

Write ten loglines for the same idea. Vary the protagonist's flaw, the antagonist, the clock. Read them cold a day later. The one you still want to *watch* — not the one that is best written — is the movie. Franklin Leonard, who runs the Black List, has said repeatedly that the projects that travel are the ones whose premise survives being retold by a stranger. Test yours by telling it to someone and seeing if they retell it correctly.

## Title craft

A title does three jobs: signals genre, is memorable, and is searchable. In a streaming/algorithm market a title that collides with ten other titles is a liability.

- **Genre signal.** *The Conjuring* reads horror. *Bridesmaids* reads comedy. *Sicario* reads danger even before you know it means "hitman."
- **Say-able and spell-able.** If people cannot say it, they cannot recommend it.
- **Ownable / searchable.** One-word common-noun titles (*Her*, *Us*, *It*) are strong on screen but weak in search — check what dominates the search results before committing.
- **Techniques:** a loaded noun (*Whiplash*), a character name (*Michael Clayton*, *Carol*), a place/time (*Chinatown*, *1917*), a promise (*No Country for Old Men*), a number (*Se7en*, *300*).
- **Working title vs. release title.** Keep a working title to protect the project; expect marketing to re-title before release. Titles are not final until the poster is.

Generate 20, kill 18. Check each against: does it signal the right genre, is it takeable in search, does it survive being said out loud in a crowded bar.

## Comps — "X meets Y"

A comp (comparable) is shorthand that lets a buyer locate your film on a map they already have. "*Jaws* in space" sold *Alien*. "*Die Hard* on a bus" is *Speed*.

### How to build an honest comp

- **Pick two films the buyer has actually seen.** A comp to an obscure title communicates nothing.
- **One comp for tone, one for structure or hook.** "*Get Out* meets *The Stepford Wives*" tells the buyer the genre engine and the social angle.
- **Comp to films from roughly the last 5–7 years** where you can. Tastes and economics move; a 1990s comp signals you are behind the market.
- **Comp to the right budget tier.** Comping a $3M indie to a $100M tentpole tells the financier you do not understand your own project.

### Dishonest comps (and why they backfire)

- **Aspirational comps.** Everyone's horror script is "the next *Hereditary*." Comping to the top 1% outcome sets a bar your film will be judged against and lose to.
- **Cherry-picked hits.** Comping only to the breakouts in a genre hides the ten films in the same lane that lost money. A financier will run the real average.
- **Genre-mismatched comps** chosen because they made money, not because they resemble your film.

Honest comping means you also pull the comps that *failed* in your lane and can say why yours is different. That is the analysis a financier respects.

## Packaging

A package is the unit that actually gets financed: material + talent + a path to money.

- **The script / underlying rights** — chain of title clean (see the legal reference). No package survives a rights defect.
- **Director** — the single biggest value-add or value-destroyer. The right director can raise the budget and attract cast; the wrong one can freeze both.
- **Cast** — usually the currency. Sales agents and financiers assign a rough value to a lead based on their recent performance in the *relevant territory and format*, not overall fame. A star who opens theatrically may mean nothing to a streamer, and vice versa.
- **Finance / sales anchor** — a sales agent's estimates, a distributor's pre-buy, a streamer's interest, soft money (tax credits, rebates, co-production treaties). Producers like Cassian Elwes and sales outfits like FilmNation, CAA Media Finance, and UTA Independent Film assemble these; the specific players and their appetites change, so verify who is active in your budget tier now.
- **The attachment order** matters: whoever you attach first sets the ceiling and the tone for everyone after. A wrong first attachment can make the right second one impossible.

Attachments give a producer negotiating power, and that power decays. Options and letters of intent expire. A package is only real while all its pieces are simultaneously available — the producer's job is to close the window before a piece falls out.

## Reading the market

Development does not happen in a vacuum — the same script is a buy in one market and a pass in another eighteen months later. What to track:

- **What is actually getting bought,** not what is winning awards. Market reports from AFM, the EFM (Berlin), and the Marché du Film (Cannes) show which genres and budget tiers are trading. Deadline and Variety report the deals as they close.
- **Glut and fatigue.** When a genre breaks out, a wave of comps follows and the market saturates. By the time the imitators reach screens, buyers have moved on. Being early to a rising lane beats being tenth into a proven one.
- **Streamer appetite shifts.** Platforms change strategy — chasing subscribers, then chasing profitability, then chasing engagement — and each shift changes what they commission and what they pay. A streamer's appetite last year is not a forecast for this year.
- **Territory-specific demand.** A star or genre that means nothing in one market can anchor a sale in another. International pre-sales can make a budget close that domestic numbers alone would not.

None of this is stable enough to memorize. Pull it live at the moment you are making the decision (see "Live data").

### Genre and development

Each genre has its own development priorities. Horror lives or dies on concept and can return outsized multiples at low budgets, which is why it dominates the low-budget lane — but the market floods fast. Comedy is the hardest to travel internationally and the hardest to sell on a logline alone (funny on the page is rare). Drama depends on cast and awards traction more than premise. Thrillers and action need a clear engine and set pieces that read in a trailer. Family and four-quadrant films need scale and brand safety. Know your genre's economics before you write, not after.

## Greenlight / market scorecard

A structured way to pressure-test a project before spending real money. Score each axis 1–5, weight, sum. This is a decision aid, not an oracle — it forces you to say *why*, on the record, before you commit.

| Axis | What you are judging | Weight | 1 (weak) | 5 (strong) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Concept / hook** | Is the logline an engine? Is there irony? Does a stranger retell it right? | 25% | Mood piece, no engine | Instantly retellable, built-in irony |
| **Castability** | Can this attract talent the market values *in this budget tier and format*? | 20% | No natural lead, no roles that draw | Multiple castable, want-to-play roles |
| **Comps' performance** | How did honest comps (hits *and* misses) actually do — pulled live? | 20% | Lane is a graveyard | Lane shows repeatable returns at this budget |
| **Budget-to-market fit** | Does the cost match what this category earns in its likely release path? | 20% | $40M for a lane that tops out at $12M | Budget sits well under realistic ceiling |
| **Timing / cultural fit** | Is the market moving toward or away from this? Any glut or fatigue? | 15% | Genre glut, fatigue signals | Rising interest, thin competition |

Scoring method:

1. Score each axis 1–5 with a one-sentence justification. No naked numbers.
2. Multiply by weight, sum to a 1–5 composite.
3. Read the composite as a conversation-starter, not a verdict: roughly, 4.0+ = pursue and package; 3.0–3.9 = fixable, name the weak axis and address it before spending; below 3.0 = the concept or the economics need rework, not a bigger budget.
4. **The lowest axis is the note.** A 4.5 concept with a 2 on budget-to-market fit is not a 3.4 project you fund — it is a great idea at the wrong price. Fix the low axis or pass.

Two hard rules the scorecard enforces:

- **Budget-to-market fit is a hard gate, not an average-able axis.** A brilliant concept at a budget its lane cannot return is still a loss. Do not let a high concept score paper over an underwater budget.
- **Comps and timing must be pulled live.** A comp average from memory is worse than none, because it feels like data. Pull the actual numbers the day you score.

## Live data

Box office, streamer license ranges, quote levels, tax-credit rates, and genre-appetite signals all move. Whenever any of them enters a decision, pull it fresh:

- Box office / comp performance: The Numbers, Box Office Mojo, and trade reporting (Variety, The Hollywood Reporter, Deadline).
- Market appetite / what is selling: festival market coverage (AFM, EFM/Berlin, Marché du Film/Cannes), Deadline and Variety deal reporting.
- Talent value by territory: recent openings and streamer viewership charts (Netflix Top 10, Nielsen streaming, Luminate) — with the caveat that raw viewership is not profit.
