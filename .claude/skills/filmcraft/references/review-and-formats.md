# Film Evaluation & Formats — Reference

Grounded in industry sources on script coverage, festival programming, film criticism, and format craft (cited at the end).

---

## PART A — How Professionals Evaluate Film Work

The core mental shift for a critic: **a fan reports a reaction ("I loved it / it was boring"); a professional diagnoses a cause and prescribes a fix ("the midpoint doesn't cost the hero anything, so the second half has no engine").** Every note below is built to force that shift.

### A1. Script coverage — how development execs and readers work

Coverage is the assembly-line filter of the industry. A junior reader ("story analyst") reads the script, writes a report, and assigns a grade so an executive can decide in 90 seconds whether to read it, pass it up the chain, or reject it. Standard structure:

| Section | Length | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Top sheet / fact block** | 1 page | Title, writer, genre, page count, locale, era, elements attached |
| **Logline** | 1 sentence | Protagonist + inciting problem + goal + the distinctive hook. A *test*: if you can't write a clean logline, the script has no spine. |
| **Synopsis** | ½–3 pages | Neutral beat-by-beat retelling of the whole plot **including the ending** (coverage always spoils — the exec won't read the script). |
| **Comments / analysis** | 1–3 pages | The actual critique: what works, what's broken, whether it's fixable, whether the company should want it. |
| **Grading grid** | ½ page | Craft categories scored, plus the overall verdict. |

**The verdict (three tiers):**
- **PASS** — the most common outcome; "needs a lot of work" / doesn't meet the company's needs. The default.
- **CONSIDER** — flawed but has redeeming value (a strong hook, great dialogue, a fresh voice); worth a second set of eyes.
- **RECOMMEND** — rare and reputation-staking; the reader personally vouches it's ready to be read by someone senior or green-lit. A reader who "recommends" a bad script loses credibility, so recommends are guarded.
- Many shops grade the **writer** separately from the **script** (PASS on script / CONSIDER on writer = "this project is dead but hire this person").

**The craft grid** — categories scored on **Excellent / Good / Fair / Poor** (or 1–10). The near-universal set:

| Category | What it grades |
|---|---|
| **Premise / concept** | Fresh, big enough, inherently dramatic? The "why this, why now." |
| **Structure / plot** | Does it escalate? Are act breaks, midpoint, and climax load-bearing? Is causality tight (this-*therefore*-that, not this-*and then*-that)? |
| **Characterization** | Do characters want something, change, behave from consistent psychology? Active or passive protagonist? |
| **Dialogue** | Distinct voices, subtext, economy — or on-the-nose exposition where everyone sounds the same? |
| **Marketability / commercial potential** | Genre, audience, comps, budget realism, castability. Separates a *good* script from a *sellable* one. |

Sometimes added: **Setting/world**, **Tone**, **Theme**, **Craft/format** (does it read professionally).

**Reader discipline (rules of the trade):**
- *Don't fill in the holes* — critique the script that exists, not the better version you imagine.
- Evaluate against **what the company would want to produce, not personal taste.** A reader who passes on a solid horror script because they dislike horror is doing the job wrong.
- Coverage is confidential and blunt — written for the executive, not the writer.

### A2. How critics and programmers judge a *finished* film

Coverage judges *potential on the page*; criticism and programming judge *what actually got made*. The lens changes from "can this be fixed" to "does this succeed on its own terms."

**Festival programmers (Sundance / Cannes / TIFF thinking).** They aren't scoring a rubric — they're **curating a room**:
- **"Did it have something to say — were we affected by it — does it start a conversation?"** A technically clean but inert film loses to a rough film that provokes.
- **Ground-breaking / a distinct voice** — *someone breaking ground with a film that stops me when I'm watching it… I want to bring this back to colleagues and talk about it.* The "split-the-room" film often has a longer festival life than the consensus-pleasant one.
- **A cut the filmmaker is proud of** — programmers won't program a film the maker is apologizing for.
- **Fit and freshness of voice over polish** — underrepresented perspectives, world-premiere status (Sundance: ~80–90% are premieres), and how the film contributes to the *overall* lineup.
- **Eligibility as a gate** — Cannes Official Selection requires production within the prior ~12 months, no prior screening outside the home country, no other international festival exposure. Rules first, art second.

**Critics (IndieWire / Sight & Sound register).** Axes a fan rarely names:
- **Vision** — a governing idea and point of view, or just competent coverage of a script?
- **Control of craft** — mise-en-scène, blocking, camera (as *meaning*, not decoration), lighting (as mood), color as theme, editing rhythm, sound design, score.
- **Does form serve content** — the central question. Style that *expresses* the story's idea beats style applied on top. A handheld camera that enacts chaos = good; handheld because it looks cool = noise.
- **Originality** — formal innovation, or a fresh angle on the familiar; predictability is the enemy.
- **Emotional truth** — do performances and moments feel earned, or manipulated/sentimental?
- **The director as conductor** — do tone, pace, and performance cohere into one unified thing, or is it a bag of good scenes?
- **Ambition vs. execution** — critics separate *reach* from *grasp*. A flawed film reaching for something huge is often graded more generously than a flawless film that risks nothing.

### A3. On-set / post feedback culture — how pros give notes to each other

The norm is **specific, actionable, ego-aware** feedback:
- **"The note behind the note."** The stated note is usually a symptom. *"I got bored in the second act"* is the sensation, not the fix — find the structural cause (passive protagonist, stakes-free midpoint) that produced it. If several people flag the *same* spot, something there is genuinely unclear — even if each proposes a different "fix."
- **Diagnose, don't prescribe (mostly).** Name the *problem*, leave the *solution* to the craftsperson whose domain it is: *"this beat doesn't land"* invites the editor's expertise; *"add a cutaway to the clock here"* steps on it.
- **Big-picture before line-level.** Structure → scene → shot → frame. Never open with "fix this transition" if the whole sequence is in the wrong place.
- **The note serves the maker's vision**, not the note-giver's taste. *What you're offering is help making their vision as clear as it can be.*
- **Specific and located.** "Beat X, page 40" beats "the middle." Editors and DPs speak in timecodes, shot numbers, frames.
- **Ego-aware delivery** — separate the person from the work; critique choices, not competence; often lead with what's working so the recipient can *keep* it.
- **Receiving notes is a skill too:** open a conversation, ask clarifying questions, don't defend reflexively — you're not obligated to take every note, only to understand the real problem underneath it.

### A4. The critique rubric — dimensions to actually score

Merges the coverage grid, critical axes, and craft-department concerns:

| Dimension | The question | "Helpful note" example |
|---|---|---|
| **Story / structure** | Escalate; causality tight; do act breaks/midpoint/climax carry weight? | "The midpoint doesn't cost the hero anything, so the second half has no new pressure." |
| **Character** | Active protagonist? Clear want vs. need? Real change? Consistent psychology? | "The hero gets *told* the theme instead of being forced to choose it under pressure." |
| **Dialogue** | Distinct voices, subtext, economy? | "Everyone explains their feelings; give the antagonist a line that hides what he wants." |
| **Visual storytelling** | Does camera/blocking/color/light carry meaning? Show vs. tell? | "The reveal is delivered in dialogue when the frame could show it — cut the line." |
| **Pacing / editing** | Where does attention sag or rush? Is the cut motivated? | "Scene 12 makes its point in 20 seconds and runs 90 — get out on the look." |
| **Sound / music** | Does sound + score build world and emotion, or paper over gaps? | "The score is telling me to cry before the scene earns it." |
| **Performance** | Truthful, specific, consistent? Serves the character or the actor? | "The grief reads 'acted'; the quieter take on line 3 was truer — use that energy." |
| **Theme** | A governing idea, dramatized (not stated)? | "The theme is announced in the toast; let the ending *prove* it instead." |
| **Originality** | Fresh premise, voice, or form — or genre-by-numbers? | "Beat-for-beat this is [comp]; what's the one choice only *you* would make?" |
| **Execution vs. ambition** | How big is the reach, how completely grasped? | "Ambitious structure, but the framing device adds confusion without payoff — earn it or cut it." |
| **Marketability** | Audience, genre clarity, budget realism, castability, comps. | "It's a $2M drama shot like a $20M one — the scope fights the fundable version." |

### A5. Helpful note vs. useless feedback — the whole game

| Useless (fan) | Helpful (professional) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "I didn't like it." | "The midpoint doesn't cost the hero anything." | Names a *located, causal* problem you can act on. |
| "It was boring." | "Scenes 8–11 all make the same point; the story stops advancing." | Converts a feeling into a diagnosis. |
| "The dialogue is bad." | "Every character has the same vocabulary; the kid and the professor sound identical." | Specific + testable. |
| "Make it more exciting." | "Give the hero something to lose before the chase, so the chase has stakes." | Prescribes a *mechanism*, not a mood. |
| "The ending didn't work for me." | "The ending resolves the plot but not the theme you set up in the toast scene." | Ties the flaw back to the film's own promises. |

**The test for any note:** does it name *where*, name *what's broken*, and imply *why it breaks the experience*? If it only reports a feeling, it's a reaction, not a note.

## PART B — Formats and How Craft Changes

Different containers make different demands. "Good" is defined by the format's constraints, not a universal standard. The governing rule: **know the one thing the format must do, and judge everything against that.**

### B1. Feature film (~80–180 min)
- **Story:** One protagonist's transformation across a sustained arc; room for subplots, B-stories, thematic layering.
- **Structure:** Three-act (or equivalent) with load-bearing act breaks, a midpoint reversal, escalating stakes, a climax that tests the theme. ~90–120 pages.
- **Budget reality:** Must be *fundable* (castable, marketable, comps that returned money) or *festival-viable* (distinct voice worth a slot). Marketability is a real craft category, not a sellout concern.
- **"Good" =** a unified vision where form serves content across two hours; character change that's *earned*; not a bag of good scenes but one coherent thing.

### B2. Short film (typically <15–20 min, often <10)
- **Story:** **One idea, earned.** One protagonist, one central conflict, minimal characters, no subplots. Rich in subtext and image, not breadth.
- **Structure:** Not a compressed feature — a different shape. Often built around a single turn, a reveal, or a moment; can open *in medias res* and end on a beat rather than a full resolution.
- **Budget reality:** Calling card / festival currency, rarely profitable. Constraint is a feature — forces discipline.
- **"Good" =** it does *one* thing and does it completely. Most common failure: trying to be a small feature (too many characters, a subplot, a saggy middle). Judge by: is there a single clean idea, and does every second serve it?

### B3. TV / streaming drama series
- **Story:** Not one arc but an *engine* that generates arcs — a premise and cast that can sustain and escalate over many hours and seasons.
- **Structure — two axes:**
  - **Episodic** ("problem of the week"): status quo resets each episode; self-contained. Reward: any-entry-point accessibility.
  - **Serialized:** episodes are chapters; each builds on the last; permanent character development; a large story told in slices. Reward: depth and momentum. Most prestige drama is serialized or hybrid.
  - Each episode still needs its own mini-structure (A/B/C stories, act breaks tuned to ad or binge rhythm) *and* must service the season arc.
- **The showrunner:** the missing role in film. One guiding vision tracks character arcs, unresolved threads, and series identity across writers and episodes so a serialized show doesn't feel random week to week.
- **Budget reality:** Amortized across episodes; standing sets/cast; the pilot must *sell the engine*, not just tell a story.
- **"Good" =** it **sustains and escalates.** A great pilot with nowhere to go is a failure of the form. Judge: does the premise generate stories; do characters deepen without breaking; does each episode both satisfy alone and advance the whole; is escalation real or repetitive?

### B4. Documentary
- **Story:** Truth-driven, but still *authored*. Cast a wide net in production — interviews, verité, archive — without a locked script.
- **Structure:** **Found in the edit.** The film is discovered by sifting hours of disparate footage for the real moments that, placed in context, build contrast, meaning, and a spine. Structure emerges from what the footage yields, not a pre-written outline.
- **Craft specifics:** Balancing pace (momentum vs. time to absorb); ethical construction (the edit can distort truth); music and sound doing narrative lifting; a "musical" approach to cutting — rhythm, motif, and flow.
- **Budget reality:** Long timelines; enormous shooting ratios; the edit is where most money, time, and the film itself live.
- **"Good" =** a coherent, honest narrative wrung from chaos; access and real moments over polish; a point of view that doesn't betray the subject. Judge the *edit* and the *access*, not the "script."

### B5. Music video
- **Story:** Optional. The remit is **visual rhythm serving the track** — mood, image, identity over plot.
- **Structure:** Driven by the song — cuts and transitions align to beat and tempo; non-linear, abstract, associative imagery is legitimate and often the point. Greatest artistic license of any format.
- **Budget reality:** Wide range; fast turnaround; storyboard-and-execute at the low end, auteur showcase at the high end.
- **"Good" =** does the visual language *amplify the song* and the artist's identity? Editing-as-choreography. Judge rhythm, image, and cohesion with the music — not three-act logic.

### B6. Commercial / ad (typically 15–60s)
- **Story:** **Sell in 30–60 seconds.** One idea, one emotional turn or hook, one clear takeaway — brand or product must land.
- **Structure:** Tight, storyboarded, pre-visualized; footage shot specifically for the cut. Setup → turn → payoff/brand, compressed. Every frame accountable to the message.
- **Budget reality:** High cost-per-second; fast post; heavy client/agency approval layers; the storyboard is close to binding.
- **"Good" =** does it communicate *and* persuade in the time given, memorably, without confusing the message? Craft is in the compression. Judge clarity, hook, and whether the idea survives the 30-second box.

### B7. Web / social short-form (vertical, seconds to ~a few min)
- **Story:** **The hook is everything** — first 1–3 seconds decide survival against the scroll. One idea, delivered fast, often built for a loop or a rewatch payoff.
- **Structure:** Front-loaded value; vertical framing; legible sound-off (captions) *and* sound-on payoff; retention-curve-driven, not three-act.
- **Budget reality:** Low-cost, high-volume, fast iteration; the platform algorithm is a co-author; disposable by design.
- **"Good" =** does it stop the scroll, hold attention to the payoff, and reward a share or rewatch? Judge hook strength, retention, idea density per second — not narrative arc.

### B8. Korean drama (K-drama)
The prestige unit is the **mini-series: one self-contained season of ~16 episodes** (also 12 for tight OTT thrillers, 20 for broadcast), each **~60–70 min**, airing **two nights a week for eight weeks** in a fixed slot. It is *built to conclude* — a closed story with a designed ending, not a renewable franchise. That's its structural superpower: it can commit to death, transformation, and a real finale.
- **The "live shoot" (생방송):** many broadcast dramas are *shot while they air* — scripts land days before broadcast. Upside: the writer can pivot the back half to audience reaction (second-lead popularity has reshaped endings). Downside: brutal crew hours and the signature K-drama failure — the **rushed, collapsed finale**. Netflix originals (Kingdom, Squid Game) are **pre-produced** by default, trading the live feedback loop for controlled quality.
- **Writer-centric:** a K-drama is a *writer's* medium — a single credited screenwriter (작가) is the brand (Kim Eun-sook, Park Ji-eun, Kim Eun-hee). Strong authorial voice, but writer-dependent quality variance.
- **OST as structure:** the soundtrack is a **narrative device**, not wallpaper — vocal ballads deployed as **leitmotifs keyed to the couple**; when the song drops, the audience is trained to feel.
- **Cliffhanger craft:** every episode ends on a hook engineered to survive the multi-day gap — the final-60-seconds reversal/reveal, frozen on a slow-motion push-in with the OST swelling.
- **Codified romance grammar (name the tropes):** the **OTP** (destined couple, the spine), the **slow burn** (deferred first kiss), the back-hug / wrist-grab / piggyback beats, **second-lead syndrome** (the gentler second lead written to make you root for the "wrong" choice), and **makjang** (막장 — deliberately over-the-top birth-secret/amnesia/revenge melodrama; critically scorned, ratings gold).
- **Genres:** romance, romcom, **sageuk** (사극, historical/period, incl. fusion sageuk), office (Misaeng), revenge (The Glory), thriller/crime (Signal, Stranger), high-concept (Kingdom). tvN/JTBC are the prestige tier above terrestrial KBS/MBC/SBS.
- **"Good" =** romance is *load-bearing to the theme* (Reply 1988 = family; The Glory = the cost of revenge), OST used as leitmotif, episode-1 details paid off by episode 16, and — the hardest thing — **an ending that lands** rather than a live-shoot rush job. **Fails =** trope-checklist romance with no thematic engine; makjang escalation to fake stakes; a strong 1–8 that sags and collapses by 16; tone-breaking PPL.

### B9. Chinese drama (C-drama)
The norm is **long — historically 40–70+ episodes** (some past 100), episodes ~45 min: a sprawling multi-arc saga, the inverse of the K-drama's tight unit. The length is mostly an **economic artifact**, and regulators are now forcing it down (NRTA capped new dramas at 40 episodes; average length fell from ~40 in 2017 to ~29 in 2024).
- **IP pipeline:** fed by China's huge **web-novel ecosystem** — studios buy **adaptation rights ("IP")** to books with proven paid readership, importing a ready fandom (e.g. *The Untamed* from a Mo Xiang Tong Xiu novel).
- **Genres:** **wuxia** (human martial-arts, jianghu honor code), **xianxia** (wuxia + immortals/cultivation/magic), **guzhuang** (古装, "costume"/period palace intrigue), and **danmei/dangai** (male-male romance novels reworked as intense **"bromance"** because same-sex romance can't be shown).
- **Censorship as a craft force (NRTA/SARFT):** bans on **time travel** (2011), ghosts/supernatural-as-real, and depicting homosexuality directly. **The workarounds are the craft:** invent a **fictional dynasty** (can't "disrespect history"), use **transmigration** or a novel-within-a-novel frame instead of literal time travel, package magic as **"cultivation"** (self-improvement, not superstition), and carry male romance as subtext.
- **Distribution/economics:** the giants are **iQiyi, Youku, Tencent (WeTV)**; monetized by the **VIP fast-pass** (free first episodes, rest paywalled, paid early-access ahead of schedule). Because licensing was **per-episode** and longer runs meant more ad slots, producers **"water-injected" (注水剧)** 30-episode stories into 60+ with flashbacks, slow-mo, and recaps — **the defining C-drama failure**. The shift to revenue-sharing and length caps is the correction.
- **"Good" =** guzhuang spectacle *plus* a story that fills its length; **fails =** bloat, filler, and sagging middles where a 24-episode story is stretched to 60.

### B10. Anthology, limited/miniseries & web series
The dividing lines are **(a) does it intend to return, and (b) at what level does the story reset.**
- **Miniseries** (broadcast-era term, ~2–5 parts) — a **one-and-done** closed story. **Limited series** (streaming-era, ~6–12) — meant as one season but may return (True Detective, Big Little Lies). The Emmys merge them as "Limited or Anthology."
- **The limited series is the modern prestige unit** — one complete story across ~6–10 hours. Structure it as a **novel, not a stretched movie or a padded season**: each episode a chapter with its own arc *and* a serialized pull, building to one finale, **no filler**. It beats a feature (no brutal compression — it can breathe and give supporting characters real arcs) and beats an ongoing series (a finite runway *frees* writers to kill/break/transform characters permanently). The limited commitment also recruits A-list film talent (Chernobyl, The Queen's Gambit).
- **Anthology — two models:** **episode-anthology** (Black Mirror — self-contained per episode, unified by a *concept spine* like technology/society, start anywhere) and **season-anthology** (True Detective, Fargo, American Horror Story — one complete story per season, new cast/setting each time). The central craft question either way: *what connects the stories?* Fail the spine and it's just unrelated shorts under a logo.
- **Web series** — episodic, **~3–15 min**, platform-native; micro-budget treated as *discipline* (few locations/cast), pacing built to grab attention before the scroll. Its extreme mutation is the **vertical micro-drama** (60–90-second episodes, cliffhanger-per-clip).

### B11. Sequels, franchises & cinematic universes
Name the forms precisely: **sequel** (story forward, same world), **prequel** (before — tension is *how we get there*, since the destination is known), **spin-off** (same universe, built on a minor/new character), **reboot** (discards continuity, starts over), **remake** (new version of the *same* film), **legacy sequel / "requel"** (jumps far down the timeline, new leads while aged originals return — Top Gun: Maverick, Creed, The Force Awakens), **crossover** (two properties in one film). Modern films blur these.
- **Good sequel vs. cash-in — "same but different":** deliver the core appeal, but not the same story in a new coat. **Escalate the *character*, not just the spectacle** — sequels are judged superior when they deepen characterization and theme, and falter when they pile on villains/explosions at the expense of it. A good sequel is a *logical continuation of the original's ideas*, never a poorly-disguised remake that erases the first film's growth (The Godfather Part II, Aliens, T2, Toy Story 3, Fury Road, Paddington 2).
- **Trilogy = three-act structure at franchise scale:** film 1 setup/origin, film 2 the **dark middle** where the hero is broken (Empire), film 3 escalation + resolution — one arc across three films, not the same arc three times.
- **Cinematic universe (the MCU model):** standalone films **plant seeds** (post-credit stingers, artifacts) that **pay off in crossover events** — serialized **Chekhov's-gun at franchise scale**; each film both a self-contained arc and a load-bearing setup. **The fatigue failure mode:** when the required-viewing list becomes **"homework rather than entertainment"** — the fix is rebalancing interconnected vs. standalone so each film works alone.
- **Failure modes to name:** **sequelitis** (diminishing returns as each installment gets bigger but drops the character work), **empty escalation** (destroy a bigger city), **universe bloat / table-setting** (the film exists to seed the next one), **retread** (a sequel that erases prior growth to re-run the plot).

**The spine across all serialized/franchise formats:** each is defined less by story than by its **economic-and-distribution container** — the K-drama by the twin-night 8-week slot, the C-drama by per-episode licensing, the limited series by prestige-talent economics, the universe by IP-portfolio logic. In every case the **signature failure is the container overriding the story** (K-drama's rushed finale, C-drama's padding, the franchise's bloat), and the **mark of craft is a story that fills its container exactly** — commits to an ending, cuts all filler, makes every unit both self-complete and load-bearing.

### B12. Animation (a production mode, not a genre — hosts every format above)
Animation runs *every* format (feature, short, series, ad, MV) but the craft differs structurally: **nothing exists until it's authored** — no camera, no captured reality, no happy accidents. Consequences for judging it:
- **The edit is the animatic.** Story, pacing, and staging are locked in the **story reel** *before* expensive animation begins (Pixar re-cuts its reel 8–9 times pre-animation). Judge the reel; a story that doesn't work there won't be fixed in render.
- **Performance is constructed, not captured.** The animator *is* the actor, building the performance over days per shot. Judge whether characters *think* and have weight, not just move — floaty motion, twinning, and dead eyes are the tells (see `references/animation-vfx-sound.md` Part A).
- **Sub-modes:** hand-drawn/2D · 3D/CG · **stop-motion** (real puppets, a few seconds a week — Laika) · **anime** (production-committee financing, expressive "limited animation" on twos/threes). Each has its own economics and aesthetic.
- **"Good" =** every frame is a *choice* that serves story, with the stillness ("ma") to let motion land — not photoreal busyness. **Fails =** technical realism with no appeal or thought; over-animation with no rest; committing to full animation before the reel works.

**Cross-format summary — the one thing each must do:**

| Format | Must do | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| Feature | Sustain one vision & earned change over ~2h | Bag of good scenes; no unifying idea |
| Short | Earn **one** idea completely | Tries to be a small feature |
| Series | **Sustain and escalate** an engine | Great pilot with nowhere to go |
| K-drama | Fill the ~16-ep closed arc; **land the ending** | Live-shoot rushed finale; makjang for fake stakes |
| C-drama | Fill the long run with story, not filler | "Water-injected" padding; sagging middle |
| Limited/miniseries | Tell one novel-shaped story, **no filler** | Stretched movie or padded season |
| Anthology | Unite the units with a **concept spine** | Unrelated shorts under a logo |
| Web / micro-drama | Grab attention before the scroll | Slow start; no reason to watch on |
| Documentary | Find the story **in the edit**, honestly | Footage never coheres; or distorts truth |
| Music video | Make image serve the **track** | Random visuals, no rhythm/identity |
| Commercial | **Sell in 30–60s**, one clear idea | Message buried or confused |
| Social short | **Hook** and hold against the scroll | Slow start; no payoff or reason to rewatch |
| Sequel/franchise | **Same but different**; escalate character | Sequelitis; empty escalation; table-setting bloat |
| Animation | Make **every frame a choice** that serves story | Floaty/thoughtless motion; realism over appeal; no *ma* |
