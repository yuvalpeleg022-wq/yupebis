# Director Sub-Skill: Abbas Kiarostami
*Load with the base `filmcraft` SKILL.md. Channel this to write, shoot, or critique in Kiarostami's mode — or to answer "how would Kiarostami do X."*

**Core obsession / worldview.** The porous line between fiction and documentary, and between the film and its own making. The journey/road as life; mortality and the choice to keep living (*Taste of Cherry*); the ethics of looking — whether cinema can or should represent real suffering (*Life, and Nothing More…*).

**Working method.** Cast non-professionals and shot in real rural locations (the Koker region). Fed lines and reactions off-camera rather than handing over a full script; often had actors *not* perform together — he'd shoot one responding to *him*, then the other separately, and assemble the "conversation" in the edit (the car two-shots). Loved the car as a moving confessional and set. Long takes, minimal crew, patient waiting for natural light and behavior. In *Ten* he mounted small dashboard cameras and largely left the actor alone.

**Signature (spottable).** Zigzag hilltop roads in extreme long shot (the recurring pathway). Cars as framing device — characters shot through windshields, landscape sliding past. Endings that dissolve the fiction (the video-coda of *Taste of Cherry* revealing the crew; the meta-layering of *Close-Up*, restaging a real fraud trial with the real people). Minimal music. Refusal to show the "big" event (the earthquake, the suicide). Direct address; a still-photographer's eye for landscape.

**Defining films.** *Where Is the Friend's House?* (1987) · *Close-Up* (1990) · *Life, and Nothing More…* (1992) · *Through the Olive Trees* (1994) · *Taste of Cherry* (1997, Palme d'Or) · *The Wind Will Carry Us* (1999); later *Ten* (2002), *Certified Copy* (2010).

**Key collaborators.** Kanoon (the children's-institute where he began making shorts); DPs Farhad Saba and Mahmoud Kalari; the Koker villagers as recurring non-actors; later Juliette Binoche (*Certified Copy*).

**The one lesson to steal.** The most powerful moment can be the one you *don't* show — off-screen space forces the viewer to complete the film, making them a collaborator. And a "conversation" scene can be built entirely in the edit from separately-shot singles.

**Weakness / honest criticism.** The conceptual games and slow duration alienate many — accusations of aridity, coldness, festival-catering "puzzle" cinema. Some Iranian critics argued his poetic rural image of Iran was an export product flattering Western tastes. The withholding can read as evasive.

Sources: Godfrey Cheshire's Criterion essays (*Close-Up*, the Koker films) · Alberto Elena, *The Cinema of Abbas Kiarostami* · Jonathan Rosenbaum & Mehrnaz Saeed-Vafa, *Abbas Kiarostami*.
